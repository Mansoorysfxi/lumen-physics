## 1.1 Introduction to Physics

Ever since human beings began to observe, think, and reason, they have wondered about the world around them. Early attempts to organize observations about natural phenomena and material things in an orderly manner resulted in the birth of a single discipline of science called **natural philosophy**.

Owing to the enormous increase in scientific knowledge by the beginning of the nineteenth century, it was found necessary to classify the study of nature into two broad branches:
- **Biological Sciences:** Dealing with living things.
- **Physical Sciences:** Dealing with non-living things (including physics, chemistry, astronomy, geology, etc.).

### Definition of Physics
> **Physics** is the branch of science that deals with the study of **matter**, **energy**, and the **relationship between them**.

Physics is an experimental science. The scientific method emphasizes the need for accurate measurement of various measurable features of natural phenomena or man-made objects.

The study of physics involves investigating fundamental questions: the laws of motion, the structure of space and time, the nature and types of forces that hold matter together, the interactions between elementary particles, and the behavior of electromagnetic radiation.

---

### The Three Frontiers of Fundamental Science
At the present time, there are three main frontiers of fundamental science:

1. **The World of the Extremely Large:** The universe itself, galaxies, stars, and cosmology. Radio telescopes gather information from the far reaches of the cosmos and have detected the remnant cosmic microwave background radiation ("firelight" of the Big Bang), which started off the expanding universe nearly 20 billion years ago.
2. **The World of the Extremely Small:** The elementary particles that constitute matter—electrons, protons, neutrons, mesons, quarks, and others.
3. **The World of Middle-Sized / Complex Matter:** From molecules at one extreme to the Earth at the other. This encompasses condensed matter, materials science, biophysics, and complex chemical/biological systems.

---

### Areas and Branches of Physics

#### Core Disciplines
- **Mechanics:** Study of motion under the action of forces.
- **Heat & Thermodynamics:** Study of temperature, thermal energy, and heat transfer.
- **Electromagnetism:** Study of electric charges, magnetic fields, and electromagnetic waves.
- **Optics:** Study of the propagation and behavior of light.
- **Sound / Acoustics:** Study of mechanical vibrations and waves.
- **Hydrodynamics / Fluid Mechanics:** Study of fluids (liquids and gases) in motion.
- **Special Relativity & General Relativity:** Spacetime geometry and gravitation at high speeds and large masses.
- **Quantum Mechanics:** Mechanics at atomic and subatomic scales.
- **Atomic Physics & Molecular Physics:** Structure and spectra of atoms and molecules.
- **Nuclear Physics:** Structure, properties, and reactions of atomic nuclei.
- **Solid-State Physics (Condensed Matter):** Structure and properties of crystalline and amorphous solids.
- **Particle Physics (High-Energy Physics):** Ultimate constituents of matter.
- **Superconductivity & Superfluidity:** Quantum macroscopic phenomena at low temperatures.
- **Plasma Physics & Magnetohydrodynamics:** Behavior of ionized gases.
- **Space Physics:** Phenomena occurring in the upper atmosphere and outer space.

#### Interdisciplinary Areas
- **Biophysics** (physical mechanisms in living systems)
- **Chemical Physics** (chemical processes from atomic/molecular viewpoints)
- **Engineering Physics** (applying physics to industrial technology)
- **Geophysics** (physics of Earth and planetary interiors)
- **Medical Physics** (diagnostic imaging, radiation therapy, MRI)
- **Physical Oceanography** (marine currents, tides, and thermal dynamics)
- **Physics of Music** (acoustics of musical instruments and sound synthesis)

## 1.2 Physical Quantities

> Any measurable physical property of a body or phenomenon is called a **physical quantity**.

A physical quantity is defined by:
1. A **numerical magnitude** (a number).
2. A proper **unit** of measurement.

For example, when the length of a rod is specified as $2.5\text{ m}$, $2.5$ represents the numerical magnitude and $\text{m}$ (metre) represents the unit.

### Characteristics of an Ideal Standard
To establish a measurement unit, a physical reference standard must be defined. An ideal standard must fulfill two principal requirements:
1. **It must be accessible.**
2. **It must be invariable (constant in space and time).**

These two requirements are frequently incompatible in practice, requiring an international scientific compromise.

### Classification of Physical Quantities
1. **Base Quantities:** The fundamental, minimum set of independent physical quantities in terms of which all other physical quantities can be defined (e.g., length, mass, time).
2. **Derived Quantities:** Quantities whose definitions and units are derived algebraically from combinations of the base quantities (e.g., speed, acceleration, force, pressure, work).

## 1.3 The International System of Units (SI)

In 1960, an international committee (the Eleventh General Conference on Weights and Measures) agreed on a universal set of definitions and standards to describe physical quantities, named the **Système International d'Unités (SI)**.

The International System (SI) is built up from three classes of units:
1. **Base Units**
2. **Supplementary Units**
3. **Derived Units**

---

### 1. Base Units
There are **seven base units** for seven fundamental physical quantities:

| Physical Quantity | Symbol | SI Base Unit | Unit Symbol | Definition Reference |
| :--- | :---: | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Length** | $l, x, r$ | metre | $\text{m}$ | Distance travelled by light in vacuum in $1/299,792,458\text{ s}$ |
| **Mass** | $m$ | kilogram | $\text{kg}$ | Standard platinum-iridium cylinder kept at BIPM, Sèvres |
| **Time** | $t$ | second | $\text{s}$ | Duration of $9,192,631,770$ periods of radiation of $^{133}\text{Cs}$ |
| **Electric Current** | $I$ | ampere | $\text{A}$ | Current producing $2 \times 10^{-7}\text{ N/m}$ force between conductors |
| **Thermodynamic Temperature** | $T$ | kelvin | $\text{K}$ | Fraction $1/273.16$ of the thermodynamic temperature of triple point of water |
| **Luminous Intensity** | $I_v$ | candela | $\text{cd}$ | Luminous intensity in a given direction of a monochromatic source |
| **Amount of Substance** | $n$ | mole | $\text{mol}$ | Amount of substance containing as many entities as atoms in $0.012\text{ kg}$ of $^{12}\text{C}$ |

---

### 2. Supplementary Units
The General Conference on Weights and Measures classified purely geometric angle quantities as **supplementary units**:

| Quantity | Unit Name | Symbol | Geometric Dimension |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **Plane Angle** | radian | $\text{rad}$ | 2-dimensional planar angle |
| **Solid Angle** | steradian | $\text{sr}$ | 3-dimensional conical angle |

#### (a) Radian ($\text{rad}$)
> The **radian** is the plane angle between two radii of a circle which cut off on the circumference an arc equal in length to the radius of the circle (Fig. 1.1 a).

$$\theta = \frac{s}{r}$$

- When arc length $s = r$, $\theta = 1\text{ rad}$.
- For a complete circle:
  $$s = 2\pi r \implies \theta = \frac{2\pi r}{r} = 2\pi\text{ rad} = 360^\circ$$
  $$1\text{ rad} = \frac{360^\circ}{2\pi} \approx 57.3^\circ$$

#### (b) Steradian ($\text{sr}$)
> The **steradian** is the solid angle (three-dimensional angle) subtended at the center of a sphere by an area of its surface equal to the square of the radius of the sphere (Fig. 1.1 b).

$$\Omega = \frac{A}{r^2}$$

- When spherical surface area $A = r^2$, $\Omega = 1\text{ sr}$.
- For a complete sphere of total surface area $A = 4\pi r^2$:
  $$\Omega_{\text{sphere}} = \frac{4\pi r^2}{r^2} = 4\pi\text{ sr} \approx 12.57\text{ sr}$$

---

### 4. Scientific Notation
Numbers are expressed in standard form using powers of ten:
$$M \times 10^n$$
Where $1 \le M < 10$ (exactly one non-zero digit to the left of the decimal point) and $n$ is an integer.

- Example 1: $134.7 = 1.347 \times 10^2$
- Example 2: $0.0023 = 2.3 \times 10^{-3}$

### 6. Conventions for Indicating Units
1. **Full name of a unit** does not begin with a capital letter even if named after a scientist (e.g., write `newton`, not `Newton`; `kelvin`, not `Kelvin`).
2. **Symbol of a unit** named after a scientist has an initial capital letter (e.g., $\text{N}$ for newton, $\text{J}$ for joule, $\text{K}$ for kelvin, $\text{W}$ for watt).
3. **Prefix** is written directly before the unit without any space (e.g., $1 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m} = 1\text{ mm}$, not `1 m m`).
4. **Combination of base units** is written with one space apart (e.g., newton metre is written as $\text{N m}$, not `Nm`).
5. **Compound prefixes are not allowed** (e.g., write $1\text{ pF}$, not `1 \mu\mu F`).
6. **Scientific notation with units:** $5.0 \times 10^4\text{ cm}$ should be expressed in base units as $5.0 \times 10^2\text{ m}$.
7. **Exponents apply to the entire prefixed unit:** When a prefixed unit is raised to an exponent, the exponent applies to the whole multiple and not the base unit alone:
   $$1\text{ km}^2 = (1\text{ km})^2 = (10^3\text{ m})^2 = 10^6\text{ m}^2$$
   $$1\text{ mm}^2 = (1\text{ mm})^2 = (10^{-3}\text{ m})^2 = 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2$$
8. **Practical recording:** Measurements in laboratory work should initially be recorded in the most convenient instrument unit (e.g., micrometer screw gauge in $\text{mm}$, calorimeter mass in $\text{g}$), but before calculation for the result, all measurements must be converted to appropriate SI base units.

## 1.4 Errors and Uncertainties

All physical measurements are uncertain or imprecise to some extent. It is very difficult to eliminate all possible errors or uncertainties in a measurement.

### Sources of Error and Uncertainty
1. **Negligence or inexperience** of a person.
2. **Faulty apparatus** (poor design, uncalibrated markings).
3. **Inappropriate method or technique**.
4. **Inadequacy or limitation of the instrument** (least count).
5. **Natural variations** of the object being measured.
6. **Imperfections of human senses**.

### Classification of Errors

| Feature | Random Error | Systematic Error |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Definition** | Repeated measurements of the quantity give different values under identical conditions. | Influences all measurements of a particular quantity equally, producing a consistent difference. |
| **Causes** | Unknown, unpredictable fluctuations in experimental environment or observer senses. | Zero error of instruments, poor calibration, incorrect scale markings. |
| **Pattern** | Unpredictable scatter on both sides ($+$ and $-$) of the mean value. | Follows a definite rule in a fixed direction (consistently too high or too low). |
| **Mitigation / Remedy** | Repeating measurements several times and taking an **arithmetic average**. | Comparing with a known standard instrument and applying a **correction factor**. |

## 1.5 Significant Figures

> In any measurement, the **accurately known digits** and the **first doubtful digit** are called **significant figures**.

A significant figure is known to be reasonably reliable. For example, if the length of a line is recorded as $12.7\text{ cm}$ using a metre rod graduated in millimetres, the digits $1$ and $2$ are accurately known, whereas $7$ is an estimated (doubtful) digit. The measurement contains **three** significant figures.

If measured with a more refined instrument with least count $0.01\text{ cm}$, the reading might be $12.70\text{ cm}$ (four significant figures). Improving instrument quality and technique extends the result to more significant figures, improving accuracy.

### Rules for Determining Significant Figures
1. **All non-zero digits (1 to 9) are significant.**  
   $12.7\text{ cm}$ has 3 significant figures; $1.428$ has 4 significant figures.
2. **Zeros between significant figures are significant.**  
   $2008\text{ kg}$ has 4 significant figures; $10.05\text{ m}$ has 4 significant figures.
3. **Zeros to the left of significant figures are NOT significant.**  
   They serve only as decimal position holders.  
   $0.00467\text{ m}$ has 3 significant figures ($4, 6, 7$).  
   $02.59$ has 3 significant figures.
4. **Zeros to the right of a significant figure in a decimal fraction are significant.**  
   $3.570$ has 4 significant figures; $7.4000$ has 5 significant figures.
5. **Zeros to the right in whole integers:**  
   The number of significant zeros is determined by the **least count** of the measuring instrument:
   - For an integer measurement of $8000\text{ kg}$:
     - If the scale least count is $1\text{ kg}$: 4 significant figures ($8.000 \times 10^3\text{ kg}$).
     - If the scale least count is $10\text{ kg}$: 3 significant figures ($8.00 \times 10^3\text{ kg}$).
     - If the scale least count is $100\text{ kg}$: 2 significant figures ($8.0 \times 10^3\text{ kg}$).

---

### Rules for Rounding Off Numbers
1. If the last digit dropped is **less than 5**, the preceding digit remains unchanged:  
   $43.74 \to 43.7$.
2. If the last digit dropped is **greater than 5**, the preceding digit is increased by 1:  
   $56.87 \to 56.9$.
3. If the digit dropped is **exactly 5**:
   - If the preceding digit is **odd**, it is rounded up (increased by 1):  
     $73.35 \to 73.4$.
   - If the preceding digit is **even**, it is left unchanged:  
     $64.45 \to 64.4$.  
     $8.250 \to 8.2$.

---

### Arithmetic Rules for Significant Figures
- **Addition & Subtraction:** The number of decimal places retained in the answer should equal the smallest number of decimal places in any of the quantities being added or subtracted:
  $$72.1 + 3.42 + 0.003 = 75.523 \to \mathbf{75.5} \quad (\text{1 decimal place})$$
- **Multiplication & Division:** The final result has no more significant figures than the factor with the smallest number of significant figures in the input data:
  $$\frac{5.348 \times 10^{-2} \times 3.64 \times 10^4}{1.336} = 1457.078 \dots \to \mathbf{1.46 \times 10^3} \quad (\text{3 significant figures, matching } 3.64)$$

## 1.6 Precision and Accuracy

### Precision
- **Precision** is determined by the **least count** or **absolute uncertainty** of the measuring instrument.
- The smaller the least count, the **more precise** the measurement.
- $\text{Absolute uncertainty} = \text{Least count of instrument}$.

### Accuracy
- **Accuracy** is determined by the **fractional uncertainty** or **percentage uncertainty**.
- The smaller the fractional or percentage uncertainty, the **more accurate** the measurement.

$$\text{Fractional Uncertainty} = \frac{\text{Absolute Uncertainty (Least Count)}}{\text{Measured Value}}$$

$$\text{Percentage Uncertainty} = \text{Fractional Uncertainty} \times 100$$

### Textbook Case Study

| Measurement | Instrument Used | Least Count (Absolute Uncertainty) | Measured Value | Percentage Uncertainty | Scientific Conclusion |
| :--- | :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **Measurement 1** | Metre rule | $\pm 0.1\text{ cm}$ | $25.5\text{ cm}$ | $\frac{0.1}{25.5} \times 100 = \mathbf{0.4\%}$ | **Less precise, but more accurate** |
| **Measurement 2** | Vernier calliper | $\pm 0.01\text{ cm}$ | $0.45\text{ cm}$ | $\frac{0.01}{0.45} \times 100 = \mathbf{2.2\%}$ | **More precise, but less accurate** |

> **Principle:** It is the relative measurement that matters. The smaller a physical quantity, the more precise the instrument that must be employed (e.g., measuring $0.45\text{ cm}$ demands a micrometer screw gauge with least count $0.001\text{ cm}$).
>
> **Core Definitions:**
> - A **precise measurement** is one that has **less absolute uncertainty**.
> - An **accurate measurement** is one that has **less fractional or percentage uncertainty/error**.

## 1.7 Assessment of Total Uncertainty in the Final Result

To evaluate the total uncertainty in a calculated result, apply the following five rules:

### 1. Addition and Subtraction: Add Absolute Uncertainties
When measurements are added or subtracted, **absolute uncertainties are added**:

$$\text{If } x_1 = 10.5 \pm 0.1\text{ cm} \quad \text{and} \quad x_2 = 26.8 \pm 0.1\text{ cm}$$
$$x = x_2 - x_1 = (26.8 - 10.5) \pm (0.1 + 0.1) = \mathbf{16.3 \pm 0.2\text{ cm}}$$

---

### 2. Multiplication and Division: Add Percentage Uncertainties
When measurements are multiplied or divided, **percentage uncertainties are added**:

#### Example: Determining Resistance from $R = \frac{V}{I}$
Given:
- $V = 5.2 \pm 0.1\text{ V} \implies \text{\% uncertainty in } V = \frac{0.1}{5.2} \times 100 \approx 2\%$
- $I = 0.84 \pm 0.05\text{ A} \implies \text{\% uncertainty in } I = \frac{0.05}{0.84} \times 100 \approx 6\%$

Total percentage uncertainty in $R$:
$$\text{Total \% uncertainty} = 2\% + 6\% = \mathbf{8\%}$$

Value of resistance:
$$R = \frac{5.2\text{ V}}{0.84\text{ A}} = 6.19\ \Omega$$
$$\text{Absolute uncertainty in } R = 6.19 \times \frac{8}{100} = 0.495\ \Omega \approx 0.5\ \Omega$$

Recorded result (to 2 significant figures, matching $V$ and $I$):
$$\mathbf{R = 6.2 \pm 0.5\ \Omega}$$

---

### 3. Power Factor: Multiply Percentage Uncertainty by the Power
If a quantity is raised to a power $n$, multiply its percentage uncertainty by $n$:

$$\text{If } Q = x^n \implies \text{\% uncertainty in } Q = n \times (\text{\% uncertainty in } x)$$

#### Example: Volume of a Sphere
$$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$$
Measured radius $r = 2.25 \pm 0.01\text{ cm}$.
- $\text{Absolute uncertainty} = 0.01\text{ cm}$
- $\text{\% uncertainty in } r = \frac{0.01}{2.25} \times 100 \approx 0.4\%$
- $\text{\% uncertainty in } V = 3 \times 0.4\% = \mathbf{1.2\%}$

Volume calculation:
$$V = \frac{4}{3} \times 3.1416 \times (2.25\text{ cm})^3 = 47.713\text{ cm}^3$$
$$\text{Absolute uncertainty in } V = 47.713 \times \frac{1.2}{100} \approx 0.57\text{ cm}^3 \approx 0.6\text{ cm}^3$$

Recorded result:
$$\mathbf{V = 47.7 \pm 0.6\text{ cm}^3}$$

---

### 4. Uncertainty in the Average of Multiple Measurements
To find the uncertainty in the average of several measurements:
1. Find the **arithmetic average** of the measured values.
2. Find the **deviation** of each measured value from the average (without regard to sign).
3. The **mean of the deviations** is the uncertainty in the average.

#### Example: Diameter of a Wire
Six micrometer readings of wire diameter ($\text{mm}$):
$$1.20, \quad 1.22, \quad 1.23, \quad 1.19, \quad 1.22, \quad 1.21$$

$$\text{Average} = \frac{1.20 + 1.22 + 1.23 + 1.19 + 1.22 + 1.21}{6} = \mathbf{1.21\text{ mm}}$$

Deviations from the average ($1.21\text{ mm}$):
$$|1.20 - 1.21| = 0.01\text{ mm}$$
$$|1.22 - 1.21| = 0.01\text{ mm}$$
$$|1.23 - 1.21| = 0.02\text{ mm}$$
$$|1.19 - 1.21| = 0.02\text{ mm}$$
$$|1.22 - 1.21| = 0.01\text{ mm}$$
$$|1.21 - 1.21| = 0.00\text{ mm}$$

$$\text{Mean Deviation} = \frac{0.01 + 0.01 + 0.02 + 0.02 + 0.01 + 0.00}{6} = \frac{0.07}{6} \approx \mathbf{0.01\text{ mm}}$$

Recorded result:
$$\mathbf{d = 1.21 \pm 0.01\text{ mm}}$$

---

### 5. Uncertainty in a Timing Experiment
For a periodic oscillation (e.g., simple pendulum):
$$\text{Uncertainty in Time Period } T = \frac{\text{Least Count of Timing Device}}{\text{Number of Vibrations } N}$$

#### Example: Pendulum Period
- Least count of stopwatch $= 0.1\text{ s}$
- Time for 30 vibrations $= 54.6\text{ s}$
- Time period $T = \frac{54.6\text{ s}}{30} = 1.82\text{ s}$
- $\text{Uncertainty in } T = \frac{0.1\text{ s}}{30} \approx 0.003\text{ s}$

Recorded result:
$$\mathbf{T = 1.82 \pm 0.003\text{ s}}$$

> **Rule of Practice:** Count a large number of swings to reduce the timing uncertainty.

## Solved Textbook Examples

### Example 1.1: Volume of a Metal Sheet
**Problem:** The length, breadth, and thickness of a sheet are $3.233\text{ m}$, $2.105\text{ m}$, and $1.05\text{ cm}$ respectively. Calculate the volume of the sheet correct up to the appropriate significant digits.

**Solution:**
- Length $l = 3.233\text{ m}$ (4 significant figures)
- Breadth $b = 2.105\text{ m}$ (4 significant figures)
- Thickness $h = 1.05\text{ cm} = 1.05 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}$ (3 significant figures)

$$\text{Volume } V = l \times b \times h$$
$$V = 3.233\text{ m} \times 2.105\text{ m} \times (1.05 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}) = 7.14573825 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}^3$$

Since thickness $1.05\text{ cm}$ has the minimum number of significant figures (3), the volume is recorded up to 3 significant figures:
$$\mathbf{V = 7.15 \times 10^{-2}\text{ m}^3}$$

---

### Example 1.2: Adding Masses with Appropriate Precision
**Problem:** The mass of a metal box measured by a lever balance is $2.2\text{ kg}$. Two silver coins of masses $10.01\text{ g}$ and $10.02\text{ g}$ measured by a beam balance are added to it. What is now the total mass of the box correct up to the appropriate precision?

**Solution:**
- Mass of box $= 2.2\text{ kg}$
- Mass of coin 1 $= 10.01\text{ g} = 0.01001\text{ kg}$
- Mass of coin 2 $= 10.02\text{ g} = 0.01002\text{ kg}$

$$\text{Total mass} = 2.2\text{ kg} + 0.01001\text{ kg} + 0.01002\text{ kg} = 2.22003\text{ kg}$$

Since the least precise measurement is $2.2\text{ kg}$ (having one decimal place), the total mass must be rounded to one decimal place:
$$\mathbf{\text{Total mass} = 2.2\text{ kg}}$$

---

### Example 1.3: Volume and Uncertainty of a Cylinder
**Problem:** The diameter and length of a metal cylinder measured with vernier callipers of least count $0.01\text{ cm}$ are $1.22\text{ cm}$ and $5.35\text{ cm}$. Calculate the volume $V$ of the cylinder and the uncertainty in it.

**Solution:**
Given:
- Diameter $d = 1.22\text{ cm}$ with least count $0.01\text{ cm}$
- Length $l = 5.35\text{ cm}$ with least count $0.01\text{ cm}$

Percentage uncertainties:
$$\text{\% uncertainty in } d = \frac{0.01\text{ cm}}{1.22\text{ cm}} \times 100 \approx 0.8\%$$
$$\text{\% uncertainty in } l = \frac{0.01\text{ cm}}{5.35\text{ cm}} \times 100 \approx 0.2\%$$

Volume of cylinder:
$$V = \frac{\pi d^2 l}{4} = \frac{3.14 \times (1.22\text{ cm})^2 \times 5.35\text{ cm}}{4} = 6.2509\text{ cm}^3$$

Total percentage uncertainty in $V$:
$$\text{Total \% uncertainty} = 2 \times (\text{\% uncertainty in } d) + (\text{\% uncertainty in } l)$$
$$\text{Total \% uncertainty} = 2(0.8\%) + 0.2\% = \mathbf{1.8\%}$$

Absolute uncertainty in $V$:
$$\Delta V = 6.2509 \times \frac{1.8}{100} = 0.1125\text{ cm}^3 \approx 0.1\text{ cm}^3$$

Recorded volume:
$$\mathbf{V = (6.2 \pm 0.1)\text{ cm}^3}$$

### Example 1.4: Checking Dimensional Correctness
**Problem:** Check the correctness of the relation $v = \sqrt{\frac{F \times l}{m}}$, where $v$ is the speed of a transverse wave on a stretched string of tension $F$, length $l$, and mass $m$.

**Solution:**
- Left-Hand Side:
  $$[v] = [L T^{-1}]$$
- Right-Hand Side:
  $$\left[\sqrt{\frac{F \times l}{m}}\right] = \left[\frac{(M L T^{-2}) \times L}{M}\right]^{1/2} = \left[L^2 T^{-2}\right]^{1/2} = [L T^{-1}]$$

Since $[L.H.S.] = [R.H.S.] = [L T^{-1}]$, the equation is **dimensionally correct**.

### Example 1.5: Derivation of the Simple Pendulum Formula
**Problem:** Derive a relation for the time period $T$ of a simple pendulum using dimensional analysis. Suppose $T$ depends on:
1. Mass of the bob ($m$)
2. Length of the pendulum ($l$)
3. Angle of swing ($\theta$)
4. Acceleration due to gravity ($g$)

**Solution:**
Express the relation as:
$$T \propto m^a l^b \theta^c g^d \implies T = \text{constant} \cdot m^a l^b \theta^c g^d \tag{1.1}$$

Substitute dimensions on both sides:
$$[T] = [M]^a [L]^b [1]^c [L T^{-2}]^d = [M]^a [L]^{b + d} [T]^{-2d}$$

Equating powers of like dimensions:
- For $[M]$: $a = 0$
- For $[T]$: $-2d = 1 \implies d = -\frac{1}{2}$
- For $[L]$: $b + d = 0 \implies b = -d = \frac{1}{2}$
- For $[\theta]$: Angle is dimensionless ($[L L^{-1}] = [L^0]$), so $c = 0$

Substitute exponents into Eq. (1.1):
$$T = \text{constant} \cdot m^0 l^{1/2} g^{-1/2} = \text{constant} \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$

Experimental measurement yields the constant as $2\pi$:
$$\mathbf{T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}}$$

### Example 1.6: Dimensions and SI Unit of Viscosity ($\eta$)
**Problem:** Find the dimensions and SI units of the coefficient of viscosity $\eta$ in Stokes' law:
$$F = 6\pi \eta r v$$

**Solution:**
$6\pi$ is a dimensionless number. Rearranging:
$$\eta = \frac{F}{r v}$$

Substitute dimensions:
$$[\eta] = \frac{[F]}{[r][v]} = \frac{[M L T^{-2}]}{[L][L T^{-1}]} = \frac{[M L T^{-2}]}{[L^2 T^{-1}]} = \mathbf{[M L^{-1} T^{-1}]}$$

SI Base Unit:
$$\text{Unit of } \eta = \mathbf{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}} \quad (\text{or } \text{N}\cdot\text{s}\cdot\text{m}^{-2} = \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s})$$

## 1.8 Dimensions of Physical Quantities

> The qualitative description of a physical quantity in terms of its fundamental constituents is called its **dimension**.

Each base quantity is considered a dimension denoted by a specific capital letter in square brackets:
- **Length:** $[L]$
- **Mass:** $[M]$
- **Time:** $[T]$

Different quantities measured in metres (length, breadth, height, diameter, displacement, wavelength, light year) all share the single dimension $[L]$.

### Dimensions of Derived Quantities
- **Velocity:** $[v] = \frac{[L]}{[T]} = [L T^{-1}]$
- **Acceleration:** $[a] = \frac{[v]}{[T]} = [L T^{-2}]$
- **Force:** $[F] = [m][a] = [M L T^{-2}]$
- **Work / Energy:** $[W] = [F][d] = [M L^2 T^{-2}]$
- **Power:** $[P] = \frac{[W]}{[t]} = [M L^2 T^{-3}]$
- **Pressure / Stress:** $[P] = \frac{[F]}{[A]} = [M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$
- **Density:** $[\rho] = \frac{[M]}{[V]} = [M L^{-3}]$

---

### Applications of Dimensional Analysis
1. **Checking Homogeneity of Equations:** An equation is valid only if every additive term on both sides has identical dimensions (Principle of Homogeneity).
2. **Deriving Physical Relationships:** Deducing power dependencies of physical quantities.

### Limitations of Dimensional Analysis
1. Cannot determine dimensionless proportionality constants (e.g., $2\pi, \frac{1}{2}$).
2. Cannot derive formulas containing trigonometric, exponential, or logarithmic functions.
3. Cannot differentiate between scalar and vector quantities sharing identical dimensions (e.g., Work and Torque).
4. Cannot uniquely solve equations if variables exceed the independent base dimensions ($M, L, T$).

## Chapter Summary

- **Physics** is the study of the entire physical world—matter, energy, and their relationships.
- The fundamental measurable properties of the physical world are **length, mass, and time**. All other mechanical quantities are derived from these.
- The **International System of Units (SI)** consists of seven base units, two supplementary units (radian and steradian), and derived units.
- **Systematic errors** arise from design flaws or calibration offsets of instruments and produce a consistent bias.
- **Random errors** arise from unknown fluctuations and are minimized by averaging multiple measurements.
- **Accuracy** is the closeness of a measured value to the true value (governed by percentage uncertainty).
- **Precision** is governed by the least count (absolute uncertainty) of the measuring instrument.
- **Significant figures** are the accurately known digits plus the first doubtful digit.
- In **multiplication and division**, the final answer cannot retain more significant figures than the least accurate input factor.
- In **addition and subtraction**, the final answer retains only as many decimal places as the term with the fewest decimal places.
- **Dimensional consistency:** Equations must be dimensionally homogeneous; only quantities of identical dimensions can be added, subtracted, or equated.

### Question 1.1
**Q:** Name several repetitive phenomena occurring in nature which could serve as reasonable time standards.  
**Ans:** Any phenomenon with constant periodicity can serve as a natural time standard:
1. Rotation of the Earth on its axis (defining the solar day).
2. Revolution of the Earth around the Sun (defining the year).
3. The periodic phases of the Moon (defining the lunar month).
4. Atomic oscillations of Cesium-133 atoms (defining the atomic second).
5. Human heartbeat (approximate physiological pulse).
6. Torsional oscillations of quartz crystals.

### Question 1.2
**Q:** Give the drawbacks of using the period of a pendulum as a time standard.  
**Ans:** The period of a pendulum is $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$. Its drawbacks are:
1. **Temperature dependence:** String length $l$ expands in summer (period increases, clock loses time) and contracts in winter (clock gains time).
2. **Location dependence:** Local gravity $g$ varies with latitude and elevation, shifting the period.
3. **Air resistance:** Damping reduces oscillation amplitude and alters the period over time.
4. **Friction at the pivot:** Causes gradual energy dissipation.

---

### Question 1.3
**Q:** Why do we find it useful to have two units for the amount of substance, the kilogram and the mole?  
**Ans:**
- **The Kilogram ($\text{kg}$)** measures **macroscopic gravitational and inertial mass**, useful for everyday commerce, manufacturing, and gross matter handling.
- **The Mole ($\text{mol}$)** measures the **number of fundamental atomic entities** ($N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles) regardless of their individual masses. This is indispensable in chemistry and particle physics where reactions occur on an atom-for-atom basis.

### Question 1.4
**Q:** Three students measured the length of a needle with a scale on which minimum division is $1\text{ mm}$ and recorded as (i) $0.2145\text{ m}$, (ii) $0.21\text{ m}$, (iii) $0.214\text{ m}$. Which record is correct and why?  
**Ans:**
- Least count of scale $= 1\text{ mm} = 0.001\text{ m}$ (three decimal places in metres).
- A physical measurement can be recorded only up to the decimal place matching the instrument's least count.
- **Record (iii) $0.214\text{ m}$ is correct.**  
  *(Reading (i) $0.2145\text{ m}$ claims precision beyond the least count, while (ii) $0.21\text{ m}$ discards valid precision).*

### Question 1.5
**Q:** An old saying states that "A chain is only as strong as its weakest link." What analogous statement can you make regarding experimental data used in a computation?  
**Ans:**

In multiplication/division, the final result retains only as many significant figures as the least accurate factor. In addition/subtraction, it retains only as many decimal places as the measurement with the fewest decimal places.

### Question 1.7
**Q:** Does dimensional analysis give any information on the constant of proportionality that may appear in an algebraic expression? Explain.  
**Ans:**
**No.** Dimensional analysis cannot determine dimensionless proportionality constants (e.g., $2\pi, \frac{1}{2}, \text{constant}$). Pure numbers have zero dimensions ($[M^0 L^0 T^0] = 1$) and cancel out of dimensional equations. Such constants must be determined experimentally or by mathematical integration.

### Question 1.9
**Q:** The wavelength $\lambda$ of a wave depends on the speed $v$ of the wave and its frequency $f$. Knowing that $[v] = [L T^{-1}]$ and $[f] = [T^{-1}]$, decide which of the following is correct: $f = v\lambda$ or $v = f\lambda$.

### Numerical 1.5
**Problem:** Find the value of $g$ and its uncertainty using $T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$ from the following measurements made during an experiment:
- Length of simple pendulum $l = 100\text{ cm} = 1.00\text{ m}$ (accuracy $1\text{ mm} = 0.001\text{ m}$).
- Time for 20 vibrations $= 40.2\text{ s}$ (accuracy $0.1\text{ s}$).

**Solution:**
1. **Time period $T$:**
   $$T = \frac{40.2\text{ s}}{20} = 2.01\text{ s}$$
2. **Value of $g$:**
   $$g = \frac{4\pi^2 l}{T^2}$$
   Using $\pi \approx 3.14$:
   $$g = \frac{4 \times (3.14)^2 \times 1.00\text{ m}}{(2.01\text{ s})^2} = \frac{39.4384}{4.0401} \approx \mathbf{9.76\text{ m/s}^2}$$
   *(Using $\pi \approx 3.1416$ gives $9.77\text{ m/s}^2$)*.
3. **Uncertainties:**
   - $\text{\% uncertainty in } l = \frac{0.1\text{ cm}}{100\text{ cm}} \times 100 = 0.1\%$
   - $\text{Uncertainty in } T = \frac{0.1\text{ s}}{20} = 0.005\text{ s}$
   - $\text{\% uncertainty in } T = \frac{0.005}{2.01} \times 100 \approx 0.25\%$
4. **Total percentage uncertainty in $g$:**
   $$\text{\% uncertainty in } g = 0.1\% + 2(0.25\%) = \mathbf{0.6\%}$$
5. **Absolute uncertainty in $g$:**
   $$\Delta g = 9.76 \times \frac{0.6}{100} \approx \mathbf{0.06\text{ m/s}^2}$$

**Answer:**
$$\mathbf{g = (9.76 \pm 0.06)\text{ m/s}^2}$$

### Numerical 1.6
**Problem:** What are the dimensions and units of gravitational constant $G$ in the formula:
$$F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

**Solution:**
$$G = \frac{F r^2}{m_1 m_2}$$

Substitute dimensions:
$$[G] = \frac{[F][r^2]}{[m_1][m_2]} = \frac{[M L T^{-2}][L^2]}{[M][M]} = \frac{[M L^3 T^{-2}]}{[M^2]} = \mathbf{[M^{-1} L^3 T^{-2}]}$$

### Numerical 1.8
**Problem:** The speed $v$ of sound waves through a medium may be assumed to depend on (a) the density $\rho$ of the medium and (b) its modulus of elasticity $E$ (ratio of stress to strain). Deduce by the method of dimensions the formula for the speed of sound.

**Solution:**
Let:
$$v = \text{constant} \cdot \rho^a E^b \tag{1}$$

Dimensions:
- $[v] = [L T^{-1}]$
- $[\rho] = [M L^{-3}]$
- $[E] = \frac{[\text{Stress}]}{[\text{Strain}]} = \frac{[M L^{-1} T^{-2}]}{[1]} = [M L^{-1} T^{-2}]$

Substitute into Eq. (1):
$$[L T^{-1}] = [M L^{-3}]^a [M L^{-1} T^{-2}]^b = [M]^{a + b} [L]^{-3a - b} [T]^{-2b}$$

Equating exponents:
1. For $[M]$: $a + b = 0 \implies a = -b$
2. For $[T]$: $-2b = -1 \implies b = \frac{1}{2}$
3. Hence: $a = -\frac{1}{2}$
4. Check for $[L]$: $-3a - b = -3(-\frac{1}{2}) - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2} - \frac{1}{2} = 1$ (matches $[L]^1$).

Substitute into Eq. (1):
$$v = \text{constant} \cdot \rho^{-1/2} E^{1/2} = \mathbf{\text{constant} \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}}}$$

### Numerical 1.9
**Problem:** Show that the famous "Einstein equation" $E = m c^2$ is dimensionally consistent.

**Solution:**
- **Left-Hand Side (Energy $E$):**
  $$E = \text{Work} = F \times d$$
  $$[E] = [M L T^{-2}][L] = \mathbf{[M L^2 T^{-2}]}$$

- **Right-Hand Side ($m c^2$):**
  $$[m] = [M]$$
  $$[c] = [L T^{-1}] \implies [c^2] = [L^2 T^{-2}]$$
  $$[m c^2] = [M][L^2 T^{-2}] = \mathbf{[M L^2 T^{-2}]}$$

Since $[L.H.S.] = [R.H.S.] = [M L^2 T^{-2}]$, the equation is **dimensionally consistent**.

### Numerical 1.10
**Problem:** Suppose we are told that the acceleration $a$ of a particle moving in a circle of radius $r$ with uniform speed $v$ is proportional to some power of $r$, say $r^n$, and some power of $v$, say $v^m$. Determine the powers of $r$ and $v$.

**Solution:**
$$a = \text{constant} \cdot r^n v^m$$

Substitute dimensions:
- $[a] = [L T^{-2}]$
- $[r] = [L]$
- $[v] = [L T^{-1}]$

$$[L T^{-2}] = [L]^n [L T^{-1}]^m = [L]^{n + m} [T]^{-m}$$

Equating exponents:
1. For $[T]$: $-m = -2 \implies \mathbf{m = 2}$
2. For $[L]$: $n + m = 1 \implies n + 2 = 1 \implies \mathbf{n = -1}$

**Answer:**
$$\mathbf{n = -1, \quad m = 2}$$
The formula for centripetal acceleration is:
$$a = \text{constant} \frac{v^2}{r} \quad \left(a = \frac{v^2}{r}\right)$$
