    Modes of Heat Transfer

    § 10.2

    Whenever two parts of an object or two touching bodies are at different temperatures, thermal energy flows naturally from the hotter region to the colder region until both reach the same temperature (thermal equilibrium).

    Heat travels from one place to another in three distinct ways. Before zooming in on conduction, see where it fits among the three modes of heat transfer:

    10.2.1

    Thermal Conduction in Solids

    SLO P-10-C-31
    Exam definition
    Definition · Thermal conduction

    Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through matter from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature by direct particle interactions, without the actual bulk movement of the material itself.

    Two ways solids pass heat along

    Why do metals conduct heat hundreds of times faster than wood, glass or plastic? Because conduction inside a solid works by two mechanisms—and non-metals only have one of them:

    1. Lattice vibrations (all solids)

    Atoms in a solid are packed closely and bound to their neighbours. Heating one end makes atoms there vibrate faster with larger swings. They bump into cooler neighbours, passing kinetic energy step-by-step down the chain—like a row of people passing buckets hand to hand. This works in every solid, but it is relatively slow.

    2. Free electrons (metals only)

    Metal atoms release outer electrons into a mobile “sea” of free (delocalised) electrons. At the hot end, these light electrons absorb thermal energy, zip through the metal lattice at high speed, and collide with cooler atoms and electrons far down the rod—delivering heat directly like express couriers.

    Quick check +10 XP

    Both copper and glass transfer heat through vibrating atoms. What gives copper its huge advantage in conducting heat?

    A surprising rule-breaker: diamond!

    Notice something wild in the table below: diamond has no free electrons (it's an electrical insulator), yet it conducts heat nearly five times better than copper! Its carbon atoms are very light and linked by ultra-stiff covalent bonds in a rigid 3D lattice, so lattice vibrations race through it at extreme speed. Meanwhile, good metallic thermal conductors (silver, copper, aluminium) are almost always good electrical conductors too—because the same free electrons carry both heat and electric charge.

    Testing conductors in the lab (SLO P-10-C-30)

    How can we demonstrate in a school laboratory which solids are good conductors and which are bad conductors—while keeping the test completely fair? The classic setup uses Jan Ingenhousz’s wax-and-pin apparatus.

    Apparatus

    A heated water trough with openings on one side, rods of identical length and diameter (copper, aluminium, iron, glass, wood), melted paraffin wax, identical metal pins (or matches), boiling water, and a stopwatch.

    Fair-test variables

    • Independent variable: material of the rod.
    • Dependent variable: time taken for each pin to fall (or distance melted).
    • Control variables: same length, thickness, wax thickness, pin spacing & bath temperature.

    Are liquids good conductors? Try the boiling-ice trick

    Drop an ice cube to the bottom of a test tube, hold it down with a bit of copper gauze, fill the tube with cold water, and heat only the top over a flame. Water at the top will boil furiously while the ice at the bottom stays frozen! Water is a very poor thermal conductor (\(k = 0.60\ \text{W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}\)). Normally water boils quickly only when heated from below—because of convection (Lesson 4), not conduction.

    Why Metal Feels Colder Than Wood (+ Uses)

    Everyday physics

    Walk into a chilly room at 20 °C. Touch the wooden door, then touch its brass or steel door handle. The metal feels ice-cold, while the wood feels almost warm—yet a thermometer says both are at 20 °C. What tricks your hand?

    Your skin does not measure an object’s actual temperature directly. Your nerve endings sense how fast thermal energy flows into or out of your skin—and how much your own fingertip cools down or heats up on contact!

    Good conductors vs insulators in daily life

    Using good conductors (metals)Using bad conductors (insulators)
    Cooking pans & kettles: copper or aluminium bases carry heat fast and evenly from the stove to food. Pan handles & kettle grips: made of wood or bakelite/plastic so heat cannot reach your hand.
    CPU heat sinks & car radiators: aluminium/copper fins pull heat rapidly away from chips and hot coolant. Winter woollens, quilts & igloos: trapped still air keeps body heat from conducting away.
    Soldering iron tips: copper tip delivers intense heat straight to the solder joint. Double-glazed windows & cooler boxes: foam walls and sealed air/argon gaps block outside heat.

    What controls how fast heat conducts?

    Imagine heat flowing steadily down a bar (or through a brick wall) of length/thickness \(L\) and cross-sectional area \(A\), with temperature difference \(\Delta T = T_{\text{hot}} - T_{\text{cold}}\) between its two faces:

    \[ \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{k\,A\,\Delta T}{L} \]
    rate of heat flow = (thermal conductivity × area × temperature difference) ÷ thickness
    • Material (\(k\)): higher thermal conductivity conducts faster (metals vs wood/air).
    • Cross-sectional area (\(A\)): wider bars or larger walls let more heat cross each second.
    • Temperature difference (\(\Delta T\)): a bigger difference between hot and cold ends pushes heat faster.
    • Length / thickness (\(L\)): thicker insulation or a longer rod slows the rate of heat flow down.

    Concept MCQ +10 XP

    Why are metals generally much better conductors of heat than non-metals?

    Short & constructed response

    SRQWhy does a metal door handle feel much colder than the wooden door on a winter morning, even though both are at the same room temperature?
    Show model answer
    • Our skin does not sense the temperature of an object directly; it senses the rate at which heat flows out of (or into) the hand.
    • Metal is a good conductor of heat (high thermal conductivity, thanks to free electrons). When your warm hand (37 °C) touches the cooler metal handle, it conducts heat away from your skin rapidly, cooling your fingertips quickly and making the handle feel cold.
    • Wood is a poor conductor (insulator). It conducts heat away from your hand very slowly, so your skin stays warmer and the wood feels neutral or mild.
    SRQWhy are cooking pots made of metals such as copper or aluminium, whereas their handles are made of wood or plastic?
    Show model answer

    The pot base is made of metal because metals are good thermal conductors (high \(k\)): heat from the burner travels rapidly and evenly through the metal to the food. The handle is made of wood or plastic because they are poor conductors (thermal insulators) with very low \(k\), blocking heat from conducting into your hand so you can lift the hot pot safely.

    CRQ · SLOJustify an experiment to distinguish between good and bad thermal conductors using rods of copper, iron, glass and wood. State what must be kept constant for a fair test.
    Show model answer

    Wax-and-pin experiment (Ingenhousz’s apparatus):

    • Take rods of copper, iron, glass and wood of identical length and diameter (control variables).
    • Coat each rod evenly with melted paraffin wax and stick identical pins at equal spacings (e.g. 5 cm apart).
    • Insert one end of all four rods into a trough of boiling water (same heat-source temperature) and start a stopwatch.
    • Observation: Pins drop off the copper rod first and farthest down its length, followed more slowly by iron, very slowly near the hot end of glass, and not at all on wood.
    • Conclusion & justification: Because dimensions, wax coating and heat source are identical, the speed at which the melting front advances depends purely on each material’s thermal conductivity—distinguishing good conductors (copper, iron) from insulators (glass, wood).
    LRQ · SLOExplain thermal conduction in all solids in terms of atomic/molecular lattice vibrations and the movement of free electrons in metallic conductors.
    Show model answer

    Conduction is heat transfer through matter from hotter to colder regions via particle interactions without bulk motion of the material.

    • In all solids (lattice vibrations): Particles are tightly bound around fixed positions. Heating one end increases the vibrational kinetic energy and amplitude of atoms there. Through inter-atomic forces, vibrating atoms collide with cooler neighbours, passing vibrational energy along the solid step by step. In non-metals (wood, glass, plastic), this slow vibration relay is the only mechanism, making them poor conductors.
    • In metals (free electrons): In addition to lattice vibrations, metals possess a sea of mobile, delocalised valence electrons. When heated, these free electrons gain kinetic energy, move rapidly through the metal lattice over long distances, and collide with cooler atoms and electrons down the rod—transferring heat far faster than lattice vibrations alone.
