    A Day at the Beach

    Textbook pp. 11 – 13
    Unit opener

    Why does a person enjoy comparatively cooler weather near the sea during the day, compared to land areas?

    Keep this question in mind. By the end of this lesson you'll be able to answer the first half of it, and Lesson 4 (sea breezes) finishes the story.

    Have you ever wondered why water takes time to boil, or why metal feels colder than wood at the same temperature? These questions lead us to explore heat: specifically, specific heat capacity and the methods of heat transfer.

    We start with specific heat capacity, the heat needed to raise a substance's temperature. Water, with its high specific heat capacity, heats slowly but retains warmth longer. That links kitchen mysteries to much bigger climate issues. After that, the unit looks at the three ways heat travels (conduction, convection and radiation), the greenhouse effect, and finally the heat inside the Earth itself.

    10.1

    Specific Heat Capacity

    Textbook p. 12

    Give 1 kg of water and 1 kg of copper exactly the same amount of heat. Their temperatures will not rise by the same amount. Different materials need different amounts of heat to warm up by one degree, and that amount has a name.

    Exam definition
    Definition · Specific heat capacity

    Specific heat capacity or specific heat of a material is the amount of heat absorbed or lost to change the temperature of 1 kg of its substance by 1 kelvin or 1 degree celsius.

    \[ c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} \]
    c = specific heat · Q = heat absorbed or lost · m = mass · ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial = change in temperature
    (10.1)

    Its SI unit is joule per kilogram per kelvin (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). Other units in use are J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ and J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹. For example, the specific heat of aluminium is 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Its value in the other units is 900 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ and 0.9 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

    Kelvin or Celsius? For ΔT it makes no difference

    A change of 1 °C is exactly the same size as a change of 1 K, so a temperature change has the same number on both scales: 75 °C − 25 °C = 50 °C = 50 K. That's why J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ and J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ are equal.

    Going from J kg⁻¹ to J g⁻¹? Divide by 1000, because each gram needs a thousandth of the heat a whole kilogram needs: 900 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ = 0.9 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

    Small c or large c?

    Some materials, like metals (for example aluminium, copper, iron and diamond), need only a small amount of heat to warm up. They have a low specific heat. Other substances, like water, need a large amount of heat to raise their temperature by one kelvin. They have a large specific heat.

    ExamplesMetals: aluminium, copper, iron, diamondWater

    BehaviourHeat up and cool down quickly. They cannot retain heat for long. (These materials are also good conductors of heat.)Absorb or lose a large amount of heat for a small change in temperature. They cannot easily be heated up or cooled down, so they retain heat.

    Heat capacity of a whole object

    Specific heat is about 1 kg of a material. Sometimes we care about a whole object instead, such as a full pot or a whole lake. The unit summary defines:

    From the unit summary · Heat capacity

    Heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of any amount of substance by 1 °C or 1 K.

    \[ \text{Heat capacity} = \frac{Q}{\Delta T} = m\,c \]
    unit: J K⁻¹ (or J °C⁻¹) · a big mass of water has an enormous heat capacity

    “Specific” means “per unit mass”

    Specific heat capacity (J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) belongs to a material: all water has c = 4190 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Heat capacity (J K⁻¹) belongs to an object: a full bucket has a bigger heat capacity than a cup, even though both are water.

    Heads-up: the summary on p. 38 states specific heat capacity “per one gram”, while §10.1 uses 1 kg. That's the same idea in a smaller unit (J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹). Use the 1 kg version (§10.1) for the definition in exams.

    Worked examples

    Example 10.1A 200 g sample of a solid metal is heated from 25 °C to 75 °C. If 3,500 joules of heat energy is required to raise the temperature of the metal, what is the specific heat of the metal?

    Answer: 350 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
    1. Given: mass m = 200 g = 0.2 kg · heat Q = 3,500 J
      Change in temperature ΔT = 75 °C − 25 °C = 50 °C = 50 K (a change in temperature is the same on the Celsius and kelvin scales).
      Required: specific heat c = ?
    2. Formula (Eq. 10.1): \[ c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} \]
    3. Put in the values: \[ c = \frac{3500\ \text{J}}{(0.2\ \text{kg})(50\ \text{K})} = \frac{3500}{10}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1} \]
    4. Answer: \[ c = 350\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1} \] The specific heat of the metal is 350 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.

    Example 10.2A half kilogram sample of aluminium (specific heat capacity c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) is heated from room temperature of 25 °C to 100 °C. How much heat is absorbed by the aluminium to raise its temperature?

    Answer: 33.75 kJ
    1. Given: m = 0.5 kg · c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ · Tinitial = 25 °C · Tfinal = 100 °C
      ΔT = 100 °C − 25 °C = 75 °C = 75 K
      Required: heat absorbed Q = ?
    2. Rearrange Eq. 10.1 to make Q the subject: \[ c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} \quad\Rightarrow\quad Q = m\,c\,\Delta T \]
    3. Put in the values: \[ Q = (0.5\ \text{kg}) \times (900\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}) \times (75\ \text{K}) \]
    4. Answer: \[ Q = 33{,}750\ \text{J} = 33.75\ \text{kJ} \] The heat required by the sample of aluminium is 33.75 kJ.

    10.1.1

    Uses of the Large Specific Heat of Water

    Textbook pp. 13 – 14

    Water has a large specific heat: about 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Because of it, water has significant uses in our daily life, and it shapes the weather of entire countries.

    (Table 10.1 lists 4190 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹; the text rounds it to 4200. Use whichever value a question gives you.)

    A. Temperature variation in land and coastal areas

    The specific heat of dry soil (800 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) is smaller than that of water: about five times smaller. That's why land areas heat up quickly in summer, with temperatures rising to about 45 °C to 50 °C, and cool down quickly in winter, falling to between 5 °C and 0 °C.

    In coastal areas the temperature variation is small. In Karachi, the average temperature varies only from about 19 °C to 32 °C. Oceans and large lakes absorb a lot of heat from the Sun during summer and store it. In winter, they release that heat into the atmosphere. This keeps the temperature moderate between summer and winter in coastal areas.

    B. Stable ocean and lake temperatures

    Lakes and oceans heat up and cool down slowly because of the high specific heat of water. This prevents big changes in the temperature of lakes and oceans, and so provides a suitable environment for aquatic life. Fish don't have to survive a 45 °C summer and a 0 °C winter the way land animals do.

    C. Human body temperature regulation

    The human body contains approximately 60 % water. A healthy body maintains its temperature at about 37 °C. Because of water's high specific heat, the body can absorb (or lose) a lot of heat with only a small change in its temperature. So your temperature stays steady on a hot day or after exercise.

    Did you know?

    The average specific heat of the human body is about 3500 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, close to water's. For a 50 kg student, raising body temperature by just 1 °C takes Q = mcΔT = 50 × 3500 × 1 = 175 000 J. That's enough to boil about half a litre of water starting from room temperature.

    D. Cooling systems and heat exchangers

    Water is used as a coolant in power plants, in different industrial processes and in the cooling systems of automobiles (radiators), as shown in Fig. 10.1. Water takes a large amount of excess heat from the machines and transfers it effectively using a heat exchanger. This prevents overheating in the machinery and keeps it working efficiently.

    Why water, and not oil or air?

    Each kilogram of water can carry away 4190 J for every degree it warms up. Air carries 721 J per kg per degree, so you'd need almost six times the mass of air to carry the same heat. That's why water-cooled engines can be compact, and why the radiator fan only has to cool the water, not the whole engine.

    E. Cooking process

    Water is good for cooking because of its high specific heat. It heats up slowly and distributes heat evenly, so food cooks without burning. Hot drinks like tea and coffee stay hot for a long time. Can you explain it?

    ThinkWhy does a cup of tea stay hot for a long time?
    Show answer

    Tea is mostly water, and water has a very large specific heat (4190 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹). To cool down by even 1 °C, each kilogram of tea must lose 4190 J of heat. The cup only loses heat to the air slowly, so it takes a long time to lose enough heat for a large temperature drop. A metal spoon of the same mass, with c only 387 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (copper), would cool about 11 times faster.

    Back to the beach: the unit opener, answered (part 1)

    The cooler daytime temperatures near the sea come from the high specific heat capacity of water. It takes much more energy to raise the temperature of water than of land, so on a sunny day the sea warms up far more slowly. The air near the coast stays cooler, influenced by the slower-warming water.

    The rest of the story is wind: the warm land and the cooler sea set up a sea breeze that pushes cool, humid air onto the shore. That's convection, and it's coming in Lesson 4.

    Short and long response

    SRQ 10How does the specific heat capacity of different materials affect their use in cookware?
    Show model answer
    • Pans and pots are made of metals with a low specific heat (aluminium, copper, iron/steel). They need little heat to get hot, so they heat up quickly and pass the heat on to the food without wasting fuel.
    • A low-c pan also cools quickly when taken off the stove. A heavy iron pan has a larger mass, so a larger heat capacity (mc), and it keeps a steadier temperature for frying.
    • Water in cooking has a high specific heat: it heats up slowly and spreads heat evenly, so food cooks gently without burning.
    LRQ 1Explain the concept of specific heat capacity in matter. Discuss various applications of water based on its high specific heat capacity.
    Show model answer

    Concept. Specific heat capacity (specific heat) of a material is the amount of heat absorbed or lost to change the temperature of 1 kg of its substance by 1 K (or 1 °C):

    \[ c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} \] SI unit: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ (also J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ and J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹).

    Materials with a low c (metals such as aluminium, copper and iron) heat up and cool down quickly and are used in heat sinks. Materials with a high c (water, 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) absorb or lose a lot of heat for a small temperature change, so they retain heat and are used to control temperature.

    Applications of water's high specific heat:

    • Coastal climate: dry soil (800 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹) has about five times less c than water, so inland areas swing from 45–50 °C in summer to 0–5 °C in winter, while Karachi stays between about 19 °C and 32 °C. Oceans store the Sun's heat in summer and release it in winter.
    • Stable lakes and oceans: water bodies heat and cool slowly, giving aquatic life a steady environment.
    • Body temperature: the body is about 60 % water, which helps keep it steady at about 37 °C.
    • Coolant: water carries excess heat away from car engines (radiators), power plants and industrial machines through heat exchangers.
    • Cooking: water heats slowly and distributes heat evenly, so food cooks without burning, and hot drinks stay hot for a long time.

    Numerical response

    QV 1Convert the specific heat of water, 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, into units of J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

    Textbook answer: 4.18 J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹
    1. Kelvin → Celsius: a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, so \( 4180\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1} = 4180\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \).
    2. Kilogram → gram: 1 kg = 1000 g, so one gram needs a thousandth of the heat: \[ \frac{4180\ \text{J}}{1\ \text{kg}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}} = \frac{4180\ \text{J}}{1000\ \text{g}\cdot{}^\circ\text{C}} \]
    3. Answer: \[ c = 4.18\ \text{J g}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1} \]

    QV 2Calculate the amount of heat given to 25 kg of water to increase its temperature by 50 °C.

    Textbook answer: 5.25 MJ
    1. Given: m = 25 kg · ΔT = 50 °C = 50 K · c (water) = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Required: Q = ?
    2. Formula: \[ Q = m\,c\,\Delta T \]
    3. Put in the values: \[ Q = 25 \times 4200 \times 50 = 5\,250\,000\ \text{J} \]
    4. Answer: \[ Q = 5.25 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J} = 5.25\ \text{MJ} \]

    QV 3A half kg block of an unknown metal is heated from 30 °C to 80 °C, requiring 19 kJ of heat energy. (a) Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. (b) If the same metal block is heated from 80 °C to 125 °C, how much additional heat energy would be needed?

    Textbook answer: 760 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, 17.1 kJ
    1. Given: m = 0.5 kg · Q = 19 kJ = 19 000 J · ΔT = 80 − 30 = 50 °C = 50 K.
    2. (a) \[ c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} = \frac{19\,000}{0.5 \times 50} = \frac{19\,000}{25} = \mathbf{760\ J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}} \]
    3. (b) New temperature change: ΔT = 125 − 80 = 45 K. The block and its c are the same.
    4. \[ Q = m\,c\,\Delta T = 0.5 \times 760 \times 45 = 17\,100\ \text{J} = \mathbf{17.1\ kJ} \]

    QV 4Calculate the change in temperature of 5 litres of water if it absorbs 8.4 MJ of heat energy. (Use specific heat of water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.)

    Textbook answer: 400 °C
    1. Litres → kilograms: 1 litre of water has a mass of 1 kg, so m = 5 kg. Q = 8.4 MJ = 8.4 × 10⁶ J.
    2. Rearrange Eq. 10.1 for ΔT: \[ \Delta T = \frac{Q}{m\,c} \]
    3. Put in the values: \[ \Delta T = \frac{8.4 \times 10^{6}}{5 \times 4200} = \frac{8\,400\,000}{21\,000} = \mathbf{400\ {}^\circ C} \] Reality check: at normal pressure, liquid water can't actually get hotter than 100 °C. Once it reaches its boiling point, the extra heat turns it into steam (latent heat, Unit 11). This question only tests the formula, so 400 °C is the expected answer.

    QV 7A hot steel rod is cooled by plunging it into cold water. The steel rod has a mass of 3 kg and is initially at 450 °C. It cools to 50 °C when placed in the water. The specific heat capacity of steel is 460 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Calculate the thermal energy (heat) lost by the steel rod as it cools to 50 °C. (Printed as a second “6” in the book.)

    Textbook answer: 552 kJ
    1. Given: m = 3 kg · c = 460 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹ · Tinitial = 450 °C · Tfinal = 50 °C.
    2. Temperature drop: 450 − 50 = 400 °C. (We use the size of the change. The heat is lost, which is what the question asks for.)
    3. \[ Q = m\,c\,\Delta T = 3 \times 460 \times 400 = 552\,000\ \text{J} = \mathbf{552\ kJ} \]

    Lesson Summary

    Definition

    Specific heat capacity

    Heat absorbed or lost to change the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K (or 1 °C).

    Equation 10.1

    \( c = \dfrac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} \;\Leftrightarrow\; Q = mc\Delta T \)

    ΔT = Tfinal − Tinitial; a change of 1 °C = a change of 1 K.

    Units

    J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹

    = J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹. Divide by 1000 for J g⁻¹ °C⁻¹ (900 → 0.9).

    Low c vs high c

    Metals vs water

    Low c: heat up and cool quickly (heat sinks). High c: slow to change, retains heat (coolant, climate control).

    Heat capacity

    \( Q/\Delta T = mc \)

    For a whole object, in J K⁻¹ or J °C⁻¹.

    Water at work

    c ≈ 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹

    Mild coasts (Karachi 19–32 °C), stable lakes, steady body temperature (37 °C), engine coolant, even cooking.
