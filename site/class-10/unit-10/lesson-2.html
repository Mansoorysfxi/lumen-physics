    10.1.2

    Measurement of Specific Heat of Solids and Liquids

    Textbook p. 15

    To find c = Q / (mΔT) we need three measurements: a mass (easy, use a balance), a temperature change (easy, use a thermometer) and a quantity of heat. You can't put heat on a balance, so how do we measure Q?

    There are different experiments which can be used to determine the specific heat of solids and liquids. The most common methods are the “method of mixtures” and the “electrical heating method”. Each one solves the “how much heat?” problem differently:

    Method of mixtures

    Let a hot object give its heat to cold water. The heat the water gains can be calculated from Q = mcΔT for water, whose c we already know.

    Electrical heating method

    Supply the heat with an electric heater. The heat it gives out is the electrical energy: Q = V × I × t.
    Key principle
    Principle of calorimetry · conservation of energy

    When hot and cold bodies are mixed, heat flows from the hot to the cold until they reach the same temperature. The heat lost by the hot bodies is equal to the heat gained by the cold bodies.

    \[ \text{Heat lost by hot bodies} = \text{Heat gained by cold bodies} \]
    valid only if no heat is lost to the surroundings, which is why calorimeters are insulated

    A (i). Unknown specific heat of a solid by the method of mixtures

    This method uses a calorimeter to determine the heat capacity of a substance. When the hot sample (e.g. a solid) is added to water in the calorimeter, heat is exchanged between them and the calorimeter. From the law of conservation of energy, the heat lost by the hot bodies is equal to the heat gained by the cold bodies. We form an equation after measuring the change in temperature of the water in the calorimeter and the masses of the substances. Then we can calculate the specific heat capacity of the sample.

    Apparatus

    A Bunsen burner or hot plate, copper calorimeter with lid, stirrer and insulating cover, a metallic solid cylinder (e.g. brass or copper), weighing machine, water, beaker, laboratory stand and two thermometers.

    Key variables (as printed in the book)

    • Independent variable: the temperature of the metal block.
    • Dependent variable: the energy supplied to the metal block by heating.
    Lab 2 · Fig. 10.2

    The virtual calorimeter

    Follow the textbook's procedure one step at a time. Your readings go into the lab notebook, and at the end Eq. 10.2 turns them into the specific heat of the solid. The default set-up is Example 10.3.

    Good technique
    1. Take an empty calorimeter of known heat capacity. Measure its mass mc with the stirrer and lid.
    2. Fill it with enough water to submerge the solid later. Weigh calorimeter + water (m); mw = m − mc.
    3. Place the calorimeter in its insulating cover, insert a thermometer and measure the water temperature T₁.
    4. Measure the mass of the solid, ms.
    5. Boil water in a beaker with the solid submerged until the solid reaches the boiling water's temperature T₂.
    6. Remove the solid, shake off the excess water and quickly place it in the calorimeter. Cover it immediately.
    7. Stir the water continuously for a uniform distribution of heat.
    8. Record the final temperature of the mixture, T₃, once it becomes constant.
    9. Calculate the rise (T₃ − T₁) for the water and calorimeter, and the fall (T₂ − T₃) for the solid.
    10. Apply the principle of calorimetry (Eq. 10.2) to find the specific heat of the solid.

    Turning the readings into cs

    From the principle of calorimetry (the law of conservation of energy), the heat given by the solid in cooling from T₂ to T₃ is equal to the heat gained by the water and the calorimeter in raising their temperature from T₁ to T₃.

    DerivationSpecific heat of a solid by the method of mixtures

    1. Heat lost = heat gained: \[ \text{Heat lost by solid} = \text{Heat gained by water} + \text{Heat gained by calorimeter} \] \[ Q_s = Q_w + Q_c \]
    2. Write each one as Q = mcΔT. The solid cools from T₂ to T₃; the water and calorimeter warm from T₁ to T₃: \[ m_s c_s (T_2 - T_3) = m_w c_w (T_3 - T_1) + m_c c_c (T_3 - T_1) \]
    3. Divide both sides by ms(T₂ − T₃) to get cs on its own: \[ c_s = \frac{m_w c_w (T_3 - T_1) + m_c c_c (T_3 - T_1)}{m_s (T_2 - T_3)} \]
    4. Take out the common factor (T₃ − T₁): \[ c_s = \frac{(m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_3 - T_1)}{m_s (T_2 - T_3)} \qquad \textbf{(10.2)} \]
    \[ c_s = \frac{(m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_3 - T_1)}{m_s (T_2 - T_3)} \]
    Result: put in the measured masses of solid, water and calorimeter, the specific heats of water and calorimeter, and T₁, T₂, T₃.
    (10.2)

    A (ii). Unknown specific heat of a liquid

    To measure the unknown specific heat capacity of a liquid, we follow the same procedure. But in this experiment we take a solid of known specific heat capacity cs, and a liquid of unknown specific heat capacity cl in the calorimeter. For this experiment, the law of conservation of energy can be written as:

    \[ m_s c_s (T_2 - T_3) = m_l c_l (T_3 - T_1) + m_c c_c (T_3 - T_1) \]

    By rearranging the equation, we get \( m_l c_l (T_3 - T_1) = m_s c_s (T_2 - T_3) - m_c c_c (T_3 - T_1) \). Hence:

    \[ c_l = \frac{m_s c_s (T_2 - T_3) - m_c c_c (T_3 - T_1)}{m_l (T_3 - T_1)} \]
    the solid is now the “known”, and the liquid is the “unknown”
    (10.3)

    Worked example

    Example 10.3A 150 g piece of an unknown solid cylinder is heated to 100 °C and then placed into a calorimeter containing 200 g of water at 25 °C. The calorimeter has a mass of 50 g and a specific heat capacity of 0.9 J/g °C. The final temperature of the system (solid, water and calorimeter) is 30 °C. Assume no heat is lost to the surroundings. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the solid.

    Answer: 0.42 J/g °C
    1. Given: ms = 150 g · mw = 200 g · mc = 50 g · T₁ = 25 °C · T₂ = 100 °C · T₃ = 30 °C · cw = 4.18 J/g °C · cc = 0.9 J/g °C.
      Required: cs = ? (in J/g °C)
    2. Principle of calorimetry (Eq. 10.2): \[ c_s = \frac{(m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_3 - T_1)}{m_s (T_2 - T_3)} \]
    3. Put in the values: \[ c_s = \frac{\{(200)(4.18) + (50)(0.9)\} \times (30 - 25)}{(150) \times (100 - 30)} \]
    4. Work it out: \[ c_s = \frac{(836 + 45) \times 5}{150 \times 70} = \frac{4405}{10\,500} \]
    5. Answer: \[ c_s = 0.42\ \text{J/g}\,{}^\circ\text{C} \] The specific heat capacity of the solid is 0.42 J/g °C (= 420 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹).

    Electrical Heating Method

    Textbook pp. 18 – 19

    The method of mixtures measures heat indirectly, through the water. Here's a more direct idea: put the heat in with an electric heater, where we can measure exactly how much energy goes in.

    Information: heat from an electric heater

    Heat generated by the heater = electrical energy supplied to the heater:

    \[ \text{Heat energy} = \text{Voltage} \times \text{current} \times \text{time} \qquad Q = V I t \]

    You will study this formula in detail in Unit 16: Current Electricity.

    B (i). Specific heat of a solid

    In this method, we measure the amount of heat transferred to a material and measure its temperature change.

    Apparatus

    A solid block or cylinder (e.g. aluminium) with a hole in it for a heater and a smaller hole for a thermometer, an immersion heater, voltmeter, ammeter and thermometer.

    Result

    Calculate the specific heat capacity of the block with Eq. 10.4, using Q = VIt and ΔT = highest temperature − initial temperature.
    1. Measure the mass of the solid block.
    2. Place an electrical immersion heater into the central hole at the top of the weighed metal block. Connect the heater with an ammeter (to measure current) and a voltmeter (to measure voltage) as shown in Fig. 10.3.
    3. Place the thermometer into the smaller hole. Add a few drops of oil into the hole so that the thermometer is surrounded by hot material.
    4. Insulate the block by wrapping it with cotton wool.
    5. Record the initial temperature of the block.
    6. Connect the heater to the power supply and turn it on for ten minutes. Record the current and potential difference.
    7. After ten minutes, turn the heater off. Record the highest temperature that it reaches (the reading rises even after the heater is turned off).
    8. Calculate the heat energy generated by the heater.
    \[ c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T} = \frac{V I t}{m\,\Delta T} \]
    (10.4)

    B (ii). Specific heat of a liquid

    Apparatus

    An immersion heater, voltmeter, ammeter, thermometer, water (or another liquid) and a calorimeter.

    Energy balance

    Energy supplied = heat gained by liquid + heat gained by calorimeter:
    \( Q_{heater} = Q_l + Q_c \)
    1. Measure the mass of the empty calorimeter (mc).
    2. Add some liquid (e.g. water) to the calorimeter and measure the mass of calorimeter plus liquid (m). The difference (m − mc) gives the mass of liquid ml.
    3. Measure the initial temperature of the calorimeter plus liquid with a thermometer.
    4. Place the electrical heater inside the liquid and connect an ammeter and a voltmeter. Switch it on. Measure the voltage and the current through the heater, as shown in Fig. 10.4.
    5. The temperature will start to rise. When it has risen by 10 °C, switch off the heater, stir the liquid well and measure the highest temperature. Find the difference between the highest and the initial temperature.
    6. Apply the law of conservation of energy (below).
    \[ Q_{heater} = m_l c_l (T_f - T_i) + m_c c_c (T_f - T_i) \] \[ c_l = \frac{Q_{heater} - m_c c_c (T_f - T_i)}{m_l (T_f - T_i)} \]
    Qheater = VIt · Ti = initial temperature · Tf = highest (final) temperature
    (10.5)

    Exercise p. 44

    These numericals use the method of mixtures. Try each one yourself before revealing the steps.

    QV 5In a container, 100 g of water at 80 °C is mixed with 200 g of water at 20 °C. What will be the final temperature of the mixture, assuming no heat loss to the surroundings (and container)? (Specific heat capacity of water = 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹.)

    Textbook answer: 40 °C
    1. Given: hot water m₁ = 100 g at 80 °C · cold water m₂ = 200 g at 20 °C · c = 4.2 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. Let the final temperature be T.
    2. Principle of calorimetry: heat lost by hot water = heat gained by cold water: \[ m_1 c\,(80 - T) = m_2 c\,(T - 20) \]
    3. c is on both sides, so it cancels: \[ 100\,(80 - T) = 200\,(T - 20) \;\Rightarrow\; 8000 - 100T = 200T - 4000 \]
    4. Solve: \[ 300\,T = 12\,000 \;\Rightarrow\; T = \mathbf{40\ {}^\circ C} \] Check: heat lost = 100 × 4.2 × 40 = 16 800 J; heat gained = 200 × 4.2 × 20 = 16 800 J ✓
    Pour it in Lab 1 ↗

    QV 6A 200 g piece of an unknown solid cylinder is heated to 110 °C and then placed into a calorimeter containing 200 g of water at 20 °C. The calorimeter has a mass of 60 g and a specific heat capacity of 0.9 J/g °C. The final temperature of the system (solid, water and calorimeter) is 35 °C. Assume no heat is lost to the surroundings. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the solid.

    Textbook answer: 890 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 0.89 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹
    1. Given: ms = 200 g · T₂ = 110 °C · mw = 200 g · T₁ = 20 °C · mc = 60 g · cc = 0.9 J/g °C · T₃ = 35 °C · cw = 4.18 J/g °C.
    2. Eq. 10.2: \[ c_s = \frac{(m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_3 - T_1)}{m_s (T_2 - T_3)} \]
    3. Put in the values: \[ c_s = \frac{\{(200)(4.18) + (60)(0.9)\}(35 - 20)}{(200)(110 - 35)} = \frac{(836 + 54) \times 15}{200 \times 75} \]
    4. Answer: \[ c_s = \frac{13\,350}{15\,000} = \mathbf{0.89\ J\,g^{-1}\,K^{-1}} = 890\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1} \] That's close to aluminium (910 in Table 10.1).
    SLOSuggest an experiment to measure the specific heat capacity of a liquid. Which readings would you take, and how would you reduce errors?
    Show model answer

    Electrical method (Fig. 10.4):

    • Weigh the empty calorimeter (mc), then with the liquid (m), so ml = m − mc.
    • Record the initial temperature Ti. Switch on the immersion heater, record V and I, and time how long it is on (t).
    • After about a 10 °C rise, switch off, stir, and record the highest temperature Tf.
    • Calculate Q = VIt and use \( c_l = \dfrac{VIt - m_c c_c (T_f - T_i)}{m_l (T_f - T_i)} \) (Eq. 10.5).

    Reducing errors: insulate the calorimeter and use a lid (less heat lost to the surroundings); stir so the temperature is uniform; read the highest temperature after switch-off; keep the temperature rise small so the liquid stays close to room temperature and loses less heat.

    (Alternatively: the method of mixtures with a hot solid of known c dropped into the liquid, using Eq. 10.3.)

    Lesson Summary

    Principle of calorimetry

    Heat lost = heat gained

    Conservation of energy, valid when no heat escapes to the surroundings.

    Mixtures · solid (10.2)

    \( c_s = \dfrac{(m_w c_w + m_c c_c)(T_3 - T_1)}{m_s (T_2 - T_3)} \)

    Hot solid (T₂) into water + calorimeter (T₁); final temperature T₃.

    Mixtures · liquid (10.3)

    \( c_l = \dfrac{m_s c_s (T_2 - T_3) - m_c c_c (T_3 - T_1)}{m_l (T_3 - T_1)} \)

    Use a solid of known c.

    Electrical · solid (10.4)

    \( c = \dfrac{VIt}{m\,\Delta T} \)

    Heater on for 10 min; record the highest temperature.

    Electrical · liquid (10.5)

    \( c_l = \dfrac{Q_{heater} - m_c c_c (T_f - T_i)}{m_l (T_f - T_i)} \)

    Switch off after a 10 °C rise, stir, record the highest temperature.

    Errors

    Heat lost to surroundings

    Mixtures: c comes out too small. Electrical: c comes out too large. Insulate, cover, transfer fast and stir.
