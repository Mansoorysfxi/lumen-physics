    10.1

    Least Distance of Distinct Vision

    Textbook p. 214

    Try this right now: hold your phone at arm's length, then slowly bring it towards your eyes. The text gets bigger… until suddenly it turns fuzzy. That point is the subject of this section.

    The normal human eye can focus a sharp image of an object on the eye if the object is located anywhere from infinity to a certain point called the near point.

    Definition
    exam definition

    The minimum distance from the eye at which an object appears to be distinct is called the least distance of distinct vision or near point.

    • This distance is represented by d and is about 25 cm from the eye.
    • If the object is held closer to the eye than this distance, the image formed will be blurred and fuzzy.
    • The location of the near point, however, changes with age.

    Tid-bit · try it at home

    If you find it difficult to read small print, make a pinhole in a piece of paper and hold it in front of your eye close to the page. You will see the print clearly.

    Why it works: the pinhole lets in only a very narrow bundle of rays. Even if the eye can't focus, that narrow bundle makes only a tiny blur spot on the retina, so the print looks sharp. The price is a dimmer image. Turn on the pinhole in Lab 1 and watch the blur spot shrink.

    10.2

    Magnifying Power and Resolving Power of Optical Instruments

    Textbook pp. 215 – 216

    An optical instrument has two jobs. It must make things look bigger (magnifying power), and it must show the details clearly (resolving power). These are two different things, and confusing them is one of the most common exam mistakes.

    Magnification

    When an object is placed in front of a convex lens at a point beyond its focus, a real and inverted image of the object is formed, as shown in Fig. 10.1 (you can rebuild it below).

    Definition
    exam definition

    The ratio of the size of the image to the size of the object is called magnification.

    \[ m \;=\; \frac{\text{size of image}}{\text{size of object}} \;=\; \frac{I}{O} \;=\; \frac{q}{p} \]
    p = distance of object from lens · q = distance of image from lens

    Apparent size & angular magnification

    The apparent size of an object depends on the angle subtended by it at the eye. Thus, the closer the object is to the eye, the greater is the angle subtended and the larger the object appears (Fig. 10.2).

    So why not just bring things really close? Because of 10.1! The maximum size of an object as seen by the naked eye is obtained when the object is placed at the least distance of distinct vision. For a lesser distance, the image formed looks blurred and the details of the object are not visible. (You proved this yourself in Lab 1.)

    To see something bigger than that, we need help from an optical device. The device makes the object subtend a bigger angle at the eye than it ever could on its own.

    Definition
    exam definition

    The magnifying power or angular magnification is defined as the ratio of the angle subtended by the image as seen through the optical device to that subtended by the object at the unaided eye.

    \[ M \;=\; \frac{\beta}{\alpha} \;=\; \frac{\text{angle subtended by image through the device}}{\text{angle subtended by object at unaided eye}} \]
    You'll use this as Eq. 10.4 for the simple microscope in §10.3.

    Resolving power

    The optical resolution of a microscope or a telescope tells us how close together two point sources of light can be so that they are still seen as two separate sources. If two point sources are too close, they will appear as one, because the optical instrument makes a point source look like a small disc or spot of light with circular diffraction fringes.

    Why zooming isn't enough

    Although the magnification can be made as large as one desires by choosing appropriate focal lengths, the magnification alone is of no use unless we can see the details of the object distinctly.

    Think of zooming into a blurry photo on your phone. It gets bigger, but no new detail appears. Physicists call this empty magnification.

    Definition
    exam definition

    The resolving power of an instrument is its ability to reveal the minor details of the object under examination.

    Resolving power is expressed as the reciprocal of the minimum angle which two point sources subtend at the instrument so that their images are seen as two distinct spots of light rather than one. Rayleigh showed that for light of wavelength λ passing through a lens of diameter D, the resolving power is given by

    \[ R \;=\; \frac{1}{\alpha_{\min}} \;=\; \frac{D}{1.22\,\lambda} \qquad\text{where}\qquad \alpha_{\min} \;=\; 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D} \]
    αmin in radians · λ = wavelength of light · D = diameter of the lens (aperture)
    (10.1)

    The smaller the value of αmin, the greater the resolving power, because two distant objects that are close together can then be seen separately through the instrument. Explore it:

    Resolving power of a diffraction grating

    In the case of a grating spectrometer, the job is different. Instead of separating two dots, we want to separate two colours (wavelengths) that are very close together. The resolving power R of the grating is defined as

    \[ R \;=\; \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_2 - \lambda_1} \;=\; \frac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} \]
    where λ ≈ λ₁ ≈ λ₂ and Δλ = λ₂ − λ₁
    (10.2)

    Thus, a grating with high resolving power can distinguish a small difference in wavelength. If N is the number of rulings on the grating, it can be shown that the resolving power in the mth-order diffraction equals the product N × m:

    \[ R \;=\; N \times m \]
    N = number of rulings (lines) on the grating · m = order of the spectrum
    (10.3)

    Q 10.1What do you understand by linear magnification and angular magnification? Explain how a convex lens is used as a magnifier.
    Show model answer

    Linear magnification is the ratio of the size of the image to the size of the object: \( m = \dfrac{I}{O} = \dfrac{q}{p} \).

    Angular magnification (magnifying power) is the ratio of the angle subtended by the image as seen through the optical device (β) to the angle subtended by the object at the unaided eye (α), when the object is placed at the near point: \( M = \dfrac{\beta}{\alpha} \).

    Convex lens as a magnifier:

    • The object is placed inside the focal point of the convex lens, and the lens is held close to the eye.
    • The lens forms a virtual, erect and magnified image on the same side as the object.
    • The lens is adjusted so that this image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision d (≈ 25 cm), so it is seen clearly.
    • The image subtends a much larger angle at the eye than the object would at 25 cm, so it looks bigger. Its magnifying power is \( M = 1 + \dfrac{d}{f} \) (derived in §10.3).
    Q 10.2Explain the difference between angular magnification and resolving power of an optical instrument. What limits the magnification of an optical instrument?
    Show model answer
    • Angular magnification tells us how much bigger an object appears through the instrument: \( M = \beta/\alpha \). It depends on the focal lengths of the lenses.
    • Resolving power tells us how well the instrument shows fine details, i.e. its ability to show two close points as separate. \( R = \dfrac{1}{\alpha_{\min}} = \dfrac{D}{1.22\lambda} \). It depends on the diameter D of the objective and the wavelength λ of light.

    What limits magnification: magnification can be made as large as we like by choosing focal lengths, but it is useful only as long as the details stay distinct. Because of diffraction, every point becomes a small disc. Once these discs overlap, more magnification only enlarges the blur. So the useful magnification is limited by the resolving power, i.e. by the aperture D of the objective and the wavelength λ of the light used. In practice, defects of lenses (aberrations) at very short focal lengths also limit it.

    Q 10.3Why would it be advantageous to use blue light with a compound microscope?
    Show model answer

    The minimum angle of resolution is \( \alpha_{\min} = 1.22\,\dfrac{\lambda}{D} \). Blue light has a shorter wavelength than most other visible light, so it gives a smaller αmin. There is less diffraction, and the resolving power \( R = 1/\alpha_{\min} \) is greater. Finer details of the specimen can therefore be seen. As the textbook puts it: "A wider objective and use of blue light of short wavelength produces less diffraction and allows more details to be seen."

    See it: in Lab 3, slide λ from red to blue and watch the discs shrink.

    Numerical problem

    10.7Sodium light of wavelength 589 nm is used to view an object under a microscope. If the aperture of the objective is 0.90 cm, (i) find the limiting angle of resolution, (ii) using visible light of any wavelength, what is the maximum limit of resolution for this microscope?

    Textbook answer: (i) 8.0 × 10⁻⁵ rad · (ii) 5.4 × 10⁻⁵ rad
    1. Write down the data (convert to SI units): \[ \lambda = 589\ \text{nm} = 589\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}, \qquad D = 0.90\ \text{cm} = 0.90\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} \]
    2. Pick the formula. The limiting angle of resolution is Rayleigh's αmin, Eq. 10.1: \[ \alpha_{\min} = 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D} \]
    3. (i) Substitute: \[ \alpha_{\min} = 1.22\times\frac{589\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}}{0.90\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}} = 7.98\times10^{-5}\ \text{rad} \approx \mathbf{8.0\times10^{-5}\ rad} \]
    4. (ii) Think first: αmin ∝ λ. The best (smallest) limit comes from the shortest visible wavelength, violet light at about 400 nm.
    5. Substitute λ = 400 nm: \[ \alpha_{\min} = 1.22\times\frac{400\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}}{0.90\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}} = 5.42\times10^{-5}\ \text{rad} \approx \mathbf{5.4\times10^{-5}\ rad} \]

    Lesson Summary

    Near point

    \( d \approx 25\ \text{cm} \)

    Least distance of distinct vision is the minimum distance from the eye at which an object appears to be distinct. Closer than this means blurred. It changes with age.

    Magnification

    \( m = \dfrac{I}{O} = \dfrac{q}{p} \)

    The ratio of the size of the image to the size of the object, which equals the ratio of the distance of the image to the distance of the object.

    Angular magnification

    \( M = \dfrac{\beta}{\alpha} \)

    The angle subtended by the image through the device ÷ the angle subtended by the object at the unaided eye.

    Resolving power (lens)

    \( \alpha_{\min} = 1.22\dfrac{\lambda}{D},\ \ R = \dfrac{1}{\alpha_{\min}} \)

    The ability to reveal minor details. Bigger D or shorter λ gives a better result.

    Resolving power (grating)

    \( R = \dfrac{\lambda}{\Delta\lambda} = N m \)

    More rulings or a higher order lets the grating separate closer wavelengths.
