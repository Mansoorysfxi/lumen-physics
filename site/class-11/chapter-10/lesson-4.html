    Astronomical Telescope

    Textbook pp. 220 – 221

    The Moon is 3,474 km wide. Yet to your eye it covers an angle of only half a degree, about the width of your little fingernail held at arm's length. A telescope makes that angle bigger.

    Definition
    exam definition

    A telescope is an optical device used for viewing distant objects.

    The image of a distant object viewed through a telescope appears larger because it subtends a bigger visual angle than when viewed with the naked eye. Initially, telescopes were used extensively for astronomical observations, so these are called astronomical telescopes.

    A simple astronomical telescope consists of two convex lenses:

    • an objective of long focal length fo, and
    • an eye-piece of short focal length fe.
    Compound microscope (L3)Astronomical telescope
    ObjectiveVery short focal lengthVery long focal length
    Eye-pieceLonger focal lengthShort focal length
    ObjectTiny and very closeHuge and very far away
    Objective's imageReal, inverted, magnifiedReal, inverted, diminished

    How it works

    • The objective forms a real, inverted and diminished image A′B′ of a distant object AB.
    • This real image A′B′ acts as the object for the eye-piece, which is used as a magnifying glass.
    • The final image seen through the eye-piece is virtual, enlarged and inverted.

    When a very distant object is viewed, the rays of light coming from any one of its points (say its top) are considered parallel. These parallel rays are converged by the objective to form a real image A′B′ at its focus.

    If we want to see the final image through the eye-piece without any strain on the eye, the eye-piece must be placed so that A′B′ lies at its focus. The rays leaving the eye-piece become parallel and the final image appears to be formed at infinity.

    Normal adjustment
    exam definition

    When the image A′B′ formed by the objective lies at the focus of both the objective and the eye-piece, the telescope is said to be in normal adjustment.

    Magnifying power in normal adjustment

    DerivationMagnifying power of an astronomical telescope (normal adjustment)

    1. Angle at the unaided eye. The object is so far away that the angle α it subtends at the eye is practically the same as at the objective, and it equals ∠A′OB′: \[ \alpha \approx \tan\alpha = \frac{A'B'}{OB'} = \frac{A'B'}{f_o} \]
    2. Angle of the final image. The angle β subtended at the eye by the final image equals ∠A′O′B′: \[ \beta \approx \tan\beta = \frac{A'B'}{O'B'} = \frac{A'B'}{f_e} \]
    3. Magnifying power is the ratio of these angles: \[ M = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{A'B'/f_e}{A'B'/f_o} \]
    4. Result: \[ M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} = \frac{\text{focal length of the objective}}{\text{focal length of the eye-piece}} \qquad \textbf{(10.8)} \]
    \[ M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} \qquad\qquad L = f_o + f_e \]
    In normal adjustment the distance between objective and eye-piece is f₀ + fₑ, which is the length of the telescope.
    (10.8)

    Gathering light

    Besides high magnifying power, astronomers face another problem: they would like to gather as much light from the object as possible. Faint stars send very little light. This difficulty is overcome by using an objective of large aperture, so that it collects a great amount of light from astronomical objects.

    The textbook's recipe

    A good telescope has an objective of long focal length (for high M = f₀/fₑ) and large aperture (to collect lots of light, and, as you saw in Lesson 1, to resolve fine detail).

    Reflecting telescopes

    For your information

    Large astronomical telescopes are of the reflecting type. They are made with specially shaped, very large mirrors used as objectives. With such telescopes, astronomers can study stars that are millions of light years away.

    Q 10.6If a person was looking through a telescope at the full moon, how would the appearance of the moon be changed by covering half of the objective lens?
    Show model answer
    • The whole moon would still be seen. Half of it does not disappear.
    • Light from every point of the moon falls on every part of the objective. The uncovered half still receives rays from all points of the moon and brings them to focus at the same image points, so the complete image is formed.
    • However, only half as much light is collected (half the area), so the image becomes dimmer: about half as bright.
    • (With a smaller aperture, diffraction also increases slightly, so very fine detail is resolved a little less well.)

    Numerical problems

    10.3A telescope is made of an objective of focal length 20 cm and an eye-piece of 5.0 cm, both convex lenses. Find the angular magnification.

    Textbook answer: 4.0
    1. Data: f₀ = 20 cm, fₑ = 5.0 cm. Assume normal adjustment.
    2. Eq. 10.8: \[ M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} = \frac{20\ \text{cm}}{5.0\ \text{cm}} = \mathbf{4.0} \]
    See it in Lab 1 ↗

    10.4A simple astronomical telescope in normal adjustment has an objective of focal length 100 cm and an eye-piece of focal length 5.0 cm. (i) Where is the final image formed? (ii) Calculate the angular magnification.

    Textbook answer: (i) infinity · (ii) 20
    1. (i) In normal adjustment, A′B′ lies at the focus of the eye-piece. Rays leave the eye-piece parallel, so the final image is formed at infinity.
    2. (ii) \[ M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} = \frac{100\ \text{cm}}{5.0\ \text{cm}} = \mathbf{20} \] (The telescope is f₀ + fₑ = 105 cm long.)
    See it in Lab 1 ↗

    10.8An astronomical telescope having a magnifying power of 5 consists of two thin lenses 24 cm apart. Find the focal lengths of the lenses.

    Textbook answer: 20 cm, 4 cm
    1. Two equations, two unknowns. In normal adjustment: \[ M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} = 5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad f_o = 5 f_e \] \[ L = f_o + f_e = 24\ \text{cm} \]
    2. Substitute the first into the second: \[ 5 f_e + f_e = 24 \;\Rightarrow\; 6 f_e = 24 \;\Rightarrow\; f_e = \mathbf{4\ cm} \]
    3. Then: \[ f_o = 5 \times 4 = \mathbf{20\ cm} \] Check: 20/4 = 5 ✓ and 20 + 4 = 24 cm ✓
    See it in Lab 1 ↗

    10.2A telescope objective has focal length 96 cm and diameter 12 cm. Calculate the focal length and minimum diameter of a simple eye-piece lens for use with the telescope, if the linear magnification required is 24 times and all the light transmitted by the objective from a distant point on the telescope axis is to fall on the eye-piece.

    Textbook answer: fₑ = 4.0 cm, diameter = 0.50 cm
    1. Focal length of the eye-piece: \[ M = \frac{f_o}{f_e} \;\Rightarrow\; f_e = \frac{f_o}{M} = \frac{96\ \text{cm}}{24} = \mathbf{4.0\ cm} \]
    2. Follow the light from a distant point on the axis. It arrives as a parallel beam 12 cm wide, fills the objective, and converges to the focus F, 96 cm behind it. After F, the cone spreads out again.
    3. Similar triangles. The eye-piece is fₑ = 4.0 cm beyond F (normal adjustment). The cone narrows from D = 12 cm to zero over 96 cm, then widens at the same rate, so at the eye-piece its width d is: \[ \frac{d}{f_e} = \frac{D}{f_o} \;\Rightarrow\; d = D\,\frac{f_e}{f_o} = 12\ \text{cm} \times \frac{4.0}{96} \]
    4. Minimum diameter of the eye-piece: \[ d = \mathbf{0.50\ cm} \] Any smaller and some of the light collected by the objective would miss the eye-piece.
    See it in Lab 1 ↗

    Lesson Summary

    What it is

    Long f₀ + short fₑ

    A telescope is an optical instrument used to see distant objects. The objective has a long focal length and the eye-piece a short one.

    Images

    A′B′ real → final virtual

    A′B′ is real, inverted and diminished, at F of both lenses. The final image is virtual, enlarged, inverted, at infinity.

    Magnifying power

    \( M = \dfrac{f_o}{f_e} \)

    Eq. 10.8, in normal adjustment. Length L = f₀ + fₑ.

    A good telescope

    Long f₀, large aperture

    A large aperture collects more light (∝ D²). The largest telescopes use mirrors as objectives.
