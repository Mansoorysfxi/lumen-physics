    Simple Microscope

    Textbook p. 217

    In Lesson 1 you hit a wall: the naked eye can't make anything look bigger than it does at the near point (25 cm). Bring it closer and it blurs. A single convex lens breaks through that wall.

    As discussed above, a converging or convex lens can be used to help the eye to see small objects distinctly. A watch maker uses a convex lens to repair watches.

    • The object is placed inside the focal point of the lens.
    • The magnified and virtual image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision d, or much farther from the lens.

    Calculating the magnification

    Let us now calculate the magnification of a simple microscope. In Fig. 10.3 (a) the object is placed at a distance d from the eye and seen directly. In Fig. 10.3 (b) a lens is placed just in front of the eye, and the object is placed in front of the lens so that a virtual image is formed at a distance d from the eye. The size of the image is now much larger than without the lens.

    If β and α are the angles subtended by the object when seen through the lens (simple microscope) and when viewed directly, then the angular magnification M is defined as

    \[ M = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} \]
    (10.4)

    Now work through the derivation one step at a time. In exams, this derivation is asked as "Derive an expression for the magnifying power of a simple microscope."

    DerivationMagnifying power of a simple microscope

    1. Start from the definition (Eq. 10.4): \[ M = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} \]
    2. Small angles are nearly equal to their tangents. From Fig. 10.3 (a), the object of size O at distance d: \[ \alpha \approx \tan\alpha = \frac{\text{size of object}}{\text{distance of object}} = \frac{O}{d} \]
    3. Through the lens (Fig. 10.3 b), the image of size I at distance q: \[ \beta \approx \tan\beta = \frac{\text{size of image}}{\text{distance of image}} = \frac{I}{q} \]
    4. The image is at the least distance of distinct vision, so q = d: \[ \beta = \frac{I}{q} = \frac{I}{d} \]
    5. Divide: \[ M = \frac{I/d}{O/d} = \frac{I}{O} \]
    6. Use linear magnification. We already know that \( \dfrac{I}{O} = \dfrac{\text{distance of image}}{\text{distance of object}} = \dfrac{q}{p} \), so \[ M = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{d}{p} \qquad \textbf{(10.5)} \]
    7. Lens formula for a virtual image (textbook form), with q = d: \[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q} \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{d} \]
    8. Multiply every term by d and rearrange: \[ \frac{d}{f} = \frac{d}{p} - 1 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \frac{d}{p} = 1 + \frac{d}{f} \]
    9. Result. The magnification of a convex lens (simple microscope) is \[ M = \frac{d}{p} = 1 + \frac{d}{f} \qquad \textbf{(10.6)} \]
    \[ M = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{d}{p} \]
    (10.5)
    \[ M = \frac{d}{p} = 1 + \frac{d}{f} \]
    d = 25 cm (near point) · f = focal length of the lens
    (10.6)

    About that minus sign

    For a virtual image, the textbook writes the lens formula as \( \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q} \) and keeps q as a positive distance. In Lesson 1's lens lab, virtual images showed a negative q instead. Both say the same thing: the image is on the same side as the object. In this lesson we follow the textbook and keep q positive.

    It is thus obvious that for a lens of high angular magnification, the focal length should be small. For example, if f = 5 cm and d = 25 cm, then

    \[ M = 1 + \frac{25\ \text{cm}}{5\ \text{cm}} = 6 \]
    The object would look six times larger when viewed through such a lens.

    Did you know?

    In the 1670s, the Dutch cloth merchant Antonie van Leeuwenhoek ground tiny glass beads into lenses with focal lengths of about a millimetre. By M = 1 + d/f, that's a magnification of roughly 250×, all from a single lens. With these simple microscopes he became the first person ever to see bacteria.

    So why not always use a tiny focal length?

    A very short focal length means a tiny, strongly curved lens. You must press it right against your eye, it shows only a very small area, and its images get coloured, distorted edges. For high magnification it's better to share the work between two lenses. That's the compound microscope, coming in Lesson 3.

    Q 10.5 (a)Describe with the help of a diagram how a single biconvex lens can be used as a magnifying glass.
    Show model answer
    • A biconvex lens of short focal length is held close to the eye.
    • The object is placed between the lens and its principal focus F.
    • Rays from the object are refracted by the lens but still diverge. The eye traces them back, and they appear to come from a point behind the object.
    • So the eye sees a virtual, erect and magnified image on the same side as the object.
    • The distance is adjusted until the image forms at the near point (d = 25 cm), where it is seen clearly and largest. The magnifying power is then \( M = 1 + \dfrac{d}{f} \).

    Q 10.7 · solve it with a ray diagram

    Q 10.7A magnifying glass gives a five times enlarged image at a distance of 25 cm from the lens. Find, by ray diagram, the focal length of the lens.

    Answer: f = 6.25 cm
    1. Draw what you're told. The image is virtual and upright, 5 times taller than the object, 25 cm from the lens, on the same side as the object.
    2. Find the object. A ray through the optical centre O goes straight, so the object's top lies on the line joining the image's top to O. The image is 5× taller, so the object must be 5× closer: \[ p = \frac{25\ \text{cm}}{5} = 5\ \text{cm} \]
    3. Draw a ray from the object's top parallel to the principal axis until it meets the lens.
    4. This ray must appear to come from the image's top. Join the image top to the point where the ray met the lens, and continue the refracted ray beyond the lens. A ray that was parallel to the axis always refracts through the focus, so where it crosses the axis is F.
    5. Measure OF: the focal length is 6.25 cm. Check with Eq. 10.6: \[ M = 1 + \frac{d}{f} \;\Rightarrow\; f = \frac{d}{M-1} = \frac{25\ \text{cm}}{5-1} = 6.25\ \text{cm}\ \checkmark \]

    Numerical problem

    10.1A converging lens of focal length 5.0 cm is used as a magnifying glass. If the near point of the observer is 25 cm and the lens is held close to the eye, calculate (i) the distance of the object from the lens, (ii) the angular magnification. What is the angular magnification when the final image is formed at infinity?

    Textbook answer: (i) 4.2 cm · (ii) 6.0 ; 5.0
    1. Data: f = 5.0 cm, d = 25 cm. For the best view, the virtual image is formed at the near point, so q = d = 25 cm.
    2. (i) Lens formula for a virtual image: \[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{f} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{25} = \frac{6}{25} \] \[ p = \frac{25}{6} = 4.17\ \text{cm} \approx \mathbf{4.2\ cm} \]
    3. (ii) Angular magnification (Eq. 10.6): \[ M = 1 + \frac{d}{f} = 1 + \frac{25}{5} = \mathbf{6.0} \] Check with Eq. 10.5: \( M = d/p = 25/4.17 = 6.0 \) ✓
    4. Image at infinity: the object must be at the focal point (p = f). Then the image subtends \( \beta = O/f \), while the naked-eye angle is still \( \alpha = O/d \).
    5. So: \[ M_\infty = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{O/f}{O/d} = \frac{d}{f} = \frac{25}{5} = \mathbf{5.0} \]

    Lesson Summary

    What it is

    One convex lens

    A simple microscope is a convex lens used to help the eye see small objects distinctly. The object is placed inside F, and a virtual, erect, magnified image forms at d (or farther).

    Definition

    \( M = \dfrac{\beta}{\alpha} \)

    Eq. 10.4: β = angle through the lens, α = angle with the naked eye at the near point.

    Magnifying power

    \( M = \dfrac{d}{p} = 1 + \dfrac{d}{f} \)

    Eqs. 10.5 and 10.6, with the image at the near point. A smaller f gives a larger M.

    Image at infinity

    \( M = \dfrac{d}{f} \)

    Object at F, relaxed eye (used in Numerical 10.1).
