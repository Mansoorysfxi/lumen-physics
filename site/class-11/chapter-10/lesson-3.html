The Compound Microscope

A single lens tops out at about 10×. To see blood cells, bacteria and the inside of a leaf, you need two lenses working as a team. The first magnifies the object, and the second magnifies that image again.

    Compound Microscope

    Textbook pp. 218 – 219

    In Lesson 2 we saw that a single lens with a tiny focal length is hard to use and gives coloured, distorted images. The solution is to split the job between two lenses.

    Whenever high magnification is desired, a compound microscope is used. It consists of two convex lenses:

    ① Objective

    The lens near the object. It has a very short focal length fo.

    ② Eye-piece

    The lens near the eye. It has a comparatively longer focal length fe.

    How it works

    • The object of height h is placed just beyond the principal focus of the objective.
    • This produces a real, magnified image of height h1 at a place situated within the focal point of the eye-piece.
    • It is then further magnified by the eye-piece, which works exactly like the magnifying glass of Lesson 2.
    • In normal adjustment, the eye-piece is positioned so that the final image is formed at the near point of the eye, at a distance d.

    Magnifying power

    The angular magnification M of a compound microscope is defined as the ratio \( \tan\theta_e / \tan\theta \), where θe is the angle subtended by the final image h2, and θ is the angle that the object of height h would subtend at the eye if placed at the near point d (Fig. 10.3 a).

    DerivationMagnifying power of a compound microscope

    1. Definition: \[ M = \frac{\tan\theta_e}{\tan\theta} \]
    2. Both angles are measured at the near point. The object (if seen directly) and the final image (normal adjustment) are both at distance d: \[ \tan\theta = \frac{h}{d}, \qquad \tan\theta_e = \frac{h_2}{d} \]
    3. Substitute: \[ M = \frac{h_2}{d} \times \frac{d}{h} = \frac{h_2}{h} \]
    4. Split it at the intermediate image h1: \[ M = \frac{h_1}{h} \times \frac{h_2}{h_1} = M_1 \times M_2 \] Here h1/h is the linear magnification M1 of the objective, and h2/h1 is the magnification M2 of the eye-piece.
    5. Use the earlier results. The objective gives linear magnification q/p (Eq. 10.5). The eye-piece is a simple microscope (Eq. 10.6): \[ M_1 = \frac{q}{p}, \qquad M_2 = 1 + \frac{d}{f_e} \]
    6. Result: \[ M = \frac{q}{p}\left(1 + \frac{d}{f_e}\right) \qquad \textbf{(10.7)} \]
    \[ M = M_1 \times M_2 = \frac{q}{p}\left(1 + \frac{d}{f_e}\right) \]
    q, p: image and object distances for the objective · fₑ: focal length of the eye-piece · d = 25 cm
    (10.7)

    It is customary to refer to the values of M as multiples of 5, 10, 40, etc. They are marked as ×5, ×10, ×40, etc. on the instrument. The total magnification is simply the objective's number times the eye-piece's number: a ×40 objective with a ×10 eye-piece gives ×400.

    Worked example

    Example 10.1A microscope has an objective lens of 10 mm focal length and an eye-piece of 25.0 mm focal length. What is the distance between the lenses, and what is its magnification, if the object is in sharp focus when it is 10.5 mm from the objective?

    Textbook answer: ≈ 233 mm · M = −220
    1. Objective alone (f = 10 mm, p = 10.5 mm). Using \( \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{f} \): \[ \frac{1}{10.5\ \text{mm}} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{10\ \text{mm}} \;\Rightarrow\; q = 210\ \text{mm} \]
    2. Eye-piece alone. The virtual image is at the least distance of distinct vision. In this form of the lens formula a virtual image distance is negative, so d = −250 mm: \[ \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{-250\ \text{mm}} = \frac{1}{25\ \text{mm}} \;\Rightarrow\; p = 22.7\ \text{mm} \]
    3. Distance between the lenses: \[ q + p = 210\ \text{mm} + 22.7\ \text{mm} \approx 233\ \text{mm} \]
    4. Magnification by each lens: \[ M_1 = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{210\ \text{mm}}{10.5\ \text{mm}} = 20.0, \qquad M_2 = \frac{-250\ \text{mm}}{22.7\ \text{mm}} = -11.0 \]
    5. Total magnification: \[ M = M_1 \times M_2 = 20 \times (-11.0) = -220 \] The negative sign indicates that the image is virtual. Check with Eq. 10.7: \( \frac{q}{p}\left(1 + \frac{d}{f_e}\right) = 20 \times \left(1 + \frac{250}{25}\right) = 220 \) ✓

    Two ways to write the lens formula

    Example 10.1 uses \( \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{f} \) and puts a minus sign on a virtual image (d = −250 mm). §10.3 instead wrote \( \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q} \) and kept q positive. Both give p = 22.7 mm. Pick one method and use it consistently in your answer.

    What limits a microscope?

    Why not just make the focal lengths tiny and get ×10,000? Two things get in the way: the colours of light, and diffraction.

    Lab 2 · Q 10.4

    Why do cheap microscopes show coloured edges?

    White light is a mix of colours, and glass bends blue a little more than red. Slide the focus and watch the edges of the specimen. Then switch to an achromatic lens.

    The second limit is diffraction, which you met in Lesson 1. The textbook puts it like this: the limit to which a microscope can be used to resolve details depends on the width of the objective. A wider objective and the use of blue light of short wavelength produce less diffraction and allow more details to be seen.

    Q 10.4One can buy a cheap microscope for use by children. The images seen in such a microscope have coloured edges. Why is this so?
    Show model answer
    • White light is a mixture of colours, and the refractive index of glass is slightly different for different colours: it is greater for violet/blue light than for red light.
    • A simple (single-glass) lens therefore bends blue light more than red, and brings the colours to a focus at different points: blue nearer the lens, red farther away. The images of different colours also have slightly different sizes.
    • When one colour is in focus, the others are slightly out of focus, so the edges of the image are fringed with colour.
    • This defect is called chromatic aberration. Cheap microscopes use simple lenses. Good microscopes use achromatic lenses (a convex crown-glass lens cemented to a concave flint-glass lens) that bring the colours to the same focus.
    Q 10.5 (b)Describe with the help of a diagram how biconvex lenses can be arranged to form a microscope.
    Show model answer

    Diagram: draw Fig. 10.4 (a) (shown above).

    • Two biconvex lenses are mounted coaxially at the two ends of a tube: an objective of very short focal length fo near the object, and an eye-piece of comparatively longer focal length fe near the eye.
    • The object AB is placed just beyond the focus of the objective. The objective forms a real, inverted and magnified image A₁B₁ inside the focal length of the eye-piece.
    • The eye-piece acts as a magnifying glass. It forms the final virtual, magnified image A₂B₂, which in normal adjustment lies at the near point (25 cm).
    • Total magnifying power: \( M = M_1 \times M_2 = \dfrac{q}{p}\left(1 + \dfrac{d}{f_e}\right) \).

    Numerical problems

    10.5A point object is placed on the axis and 3.6 cm from a thin convex lens of focal length 3.0 cm. A second thin convex lens of focal length 16.0 cm is placed coaxial with the first and 26.0 cm from it, on the side away from the object. Find the position of the final image produced by the two lenses.

    Textbook answer: 16 cm from the second lens
    1. Strategy: the image made by the first lens becomes the object for the second lens. Treat the lenses one at a time.
    2. First lens (f₁ = 3.0 cm, p₁ = 3.6 cm): \[ \frac{1}{q_1} = \frac{1}{f_1} - \frac{1}{p_1} = \frac{1}{3.0} - \frac{1}{3.6} = \frac{1}{18} \;\Rightarrow\; q_1 = 18\ \text{cm} \] This is a real image, 18 cm beyond the first lens.
    3. Object for the second lens: the lenses are 26.0 cm apart, so \[ p_2 = 26.0 - 18 = 8.0\ \text{cm} \] This is inside f₂ = 16 cm, so lens 2 acts as a magnifying glass and the final image is virtual.
    4. Second lens, using the virtual-image form \( \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{p} - \frac{1}{q} \): \[ \frac{1}{q_2} = \frac{1}{p_2} - \frac{1}{f_2} = \frac{1}{8} - \frac{1}{16} = \frac{1}{16} \;\Rightarrow\; q_2 = 16\ \text{cm} \]
    5. Answer: the final image is virtual, 16 cm from the second lens, on the same side as the first lens (towards the object).

    10.6A compound microscope has lenses of focal length 1.0 cm and 3.0 cm. An object is placed 1.2 cm from the object lens. If a virtual image is formed 25 cm from the eye, calculate the separation of the lenses and the magnification of the instrument.

    Textbook answer: 8.7 cm, 47
    1. Data: fo = 1.0 cm, fe = 3.0 cm, p = 1.2 cm, final virtual image at d = 25 cm (eye-piece close to the eye).
    2. Objective: \[ \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{f_o} - \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1}{1.0} - \frac{1}{1.2} = \frac{1}{6} \;\Rightarrow\; q = 6.0\ \text{cm}, \qquad M_1 = \frac{q}{p} = \frac{6.0}{1.2} = 5.0 \]
    3. Eye-piece (virtual image at 25 cm): \[ \frac{1}{p_e} = \frac{1}{f_e} + \frac{1}{d} = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{25} = \frac{28}{75} \;\Rightarrow\; p_e = 2.68\ \text{cm} \]
    4. Separation of the lenses: \[ L = q + p_e = 6.0 + 2.68 = 8.68\ \text{cm} \approx \mathbf{8.7\ cm} \]
    5. Magnification (Eq. 10.7): \[ M = \frac{q}{p}\left(1 + \frac{d}{f_e}\right) = 5.0 \times \left(1 + \frac{25}{3}\right) = 5.0 \times 9.33 = 46.7 \approx \mathbf{47} \]

    Lesson Summary

    What it is

    Objective + eye-piece

    Two convex lenses: an objective of very short focal length and an eye-piece of moderate focal length.

    Image formation

    Real A₁B₁ → virtual A₂B₂

    Object just beyond Fo. A₁B₁ is real, inverted and magnified, inside Fe. The final image is at the near point.

    Magnifying power

    \( M = \dfrac{q}{p}\left(1 + \dfrac{d}{f_e}\right) \)

    Eq. 10.7: M = M₁ × M₂. Markings like ×10 and ×40 multiply.

    Limits

    Colour & diffraction

    Simple lenses give coloured edges (chromatic aberration). A wider objective and blue light resolve more detail.
